The shop at the corner of Pershing and Jackson in University City, Missouri, is about 300 square feet. It sits next to a taco restaurant, a short walk from Washington University in St. Louis, and its shelves are, in St. Louis Magazine's words, tightly curated.

Debra Kennard, 57, owns it, runs it and is its only employee. She opened Gold Shoe Books in September.

## The plans that did not add up

Kennard grew up in St. Louis, worked in advertising in Chicago, then came home, raised a family and took a job at WashU. Over the years she drew up business plans for a bookshop. None of them worked financially.

What changed was partly her and partly luck. "I got to the point where I was like, 'I feel like I'm doing the same thing over and over again. I'm ready for a new adventure,'" she told St. Louis Magazine.

This spring, walking from home to campus, she noticed the vacant storefront. "It is literally just a story of right time, right place," she said. The magazine reported that it all came together in about three weeks.

## What sells, and what she learned

She prefers literary fiction, but she stocks romance and other popular genres too, and she has learned what her corner of the city buys. Romance is not what turns over most, she told the magazine; this close to WashU, her customers know the books on the Booker Prize list. The young adult section, she said, is one the customers have built, and a framed sign on the wall asks: "What would you like to see at the store?"

"It's so much relationship building," she said. She enjoys hearing what her customers are excited to read and what they are looking for.

The name comes from *The Country Bunny and the Little Gold Shoes*, a picture book about a bunny with a dream who gets busy raising her children first. "She couldn't do exactly what she wanted then, but 'not now' doesn't mean never," she said.

Six weeks in, people were still walking in to tell her how glad they were the shop was there.

## Why this matters

Kennard is part of a bigger turn. The American Booksellers Association's membership grew by more than 500 in a year, to 3,417 members with 3,783 locations, the Associated Press reported in May: nearly three times the level of a decade earlier. In the UK and Ireland, the Booksellers Association counted 1,086 independent bookshops in 2026, up from 1,025 a year before and the highest since 2012, according to Shelf Awareness.

It is not an easy living. The AP described independent bookselling as rarely a way to get rich, and the ABA's chief executive, Allison Hill, told the agency that "even a small decrease in sales can make or break a bookstore's year in an industry with paper-thin margins." That is exactly why Kennard's tiny shop is instructive: a small space, no staff to pay and stock chosen for the street outside keep the sums within reach.

## How to try it

You do not have to begin with a lease. Kelley Hartnett, 55, opened Double Dog Bookshop in Wentzville, Missouri, in a converted cargo trailer before taking a storefront, the AP reported. A market stall, a pop-up or a shelf in someone else's café can tell you what sells before you commit.

Keep your plan current, even if the numbers do not work yet: Kennard's plans were ready when the right shop appeared.
