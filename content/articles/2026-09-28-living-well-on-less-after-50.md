There is a version of "living on less" that means going without, and a version that means knowing what comes in, claiming everything you are owed, and refusing to overpay for the boring things. This is about the second one. It is general information, not financial advice; a regulated adviser or the free services below can look at your own situation.

## Step one: a budget you actually believe

On a fixed income the budget is the instrument panel, and it needs real numbers. MoneyHelper (the UK's government-backed guidance service) has a free online Budget Planner that "adds up all your income and outgoings, and then shows you what's left over", sorted into categories. Have pension statements, bank statements and bills to hand; it then points you to guidance depending on whether you have money left over, are breaking even or are overspending. Citizens Advice recommends the same planner, with the useful instruction to use accurate figures and count everything, including money that goes to family.

In the US, AARP's personal finance section offers "retirement, debt, and savings tools to help you reach your goals" and a "CA$H Hacks Challenge" described as "your roadmap to smarter spending and saving". Any tool will do; the point is to use one, with real figures, once. And do not forget the annual ambushes, car insurance, the boiler, Christmas: divide them by twelve and put them in the monthly column.

## Step two: claim what you are entitled to

This is where the real money often is. Age UK says plainly that "billions of pounds of benefits go unclaimed", and its Pension Credit page adds: "You have nothing to lose by applying, and potentially a lot to gain – and if you've previously been turned down, you can still make a new claim each year."

**Pension Credit (UK).** Gov.uk describes it as "extra money to help with your living costs if you're over State Pension age and on a low income", separate from your State Pension (the UK's government pension; Social Security is the US equivalent). Guarantee Credit tops your weekly income up to £238 if you are single or £363.25 as a couple, and "you can get Pension Credit even if you have other income, savings or own your own home". It is also a gateway: claiming it can unlock Housing Benefit, Council Tax Reduction, Cold Weather Payments, help with NHS (the UK health service) dental treatment and a free TV licence at 75. Even a small award can be worth far more than its face value.

Age UK's free benefits calculator gives full estimates for eight major benefits, including Pension Credit and Council Tax Support, and flags roughly twenty more, such as Attendance Allowance, where you "might be eligible". Treat it as an estimate. If you would rather talk to a person, the Age UK Advice Line (0800 678 1602, 8am to 7pm daily) will do a benefits check by phone; Citizens Advice's Adviceline for England is 0800 144 8848.

**SNAP, Medicare Savings Programs and Extra Help (US).** SNAP "can help you pay for food if you have a low income"; states set the rules and you apply through your state or local SNAP office. Medicare Savings Programs are state-run and can pay Part B premiums, and in the QMB programme deductibles and copayments too; federal income limits for 2026 start at $1,350 a month for an individual, but Medicare.gov notes "you may still qualify for these programs in your state even if your income or resources are higher than the federal limits listed". Extra Help, run through Social Security, lowers Part D prescription costs for people with limited income and resources.

## Step three: find the leaks

Once the income side is right, the bills are where money quietly goes missing. MoneyHelper's household-bills guide is a good checklist:

- **Energy.** Compare deals against the Ofgem price cap and consider paying by Direct Debit. Check whether you qualify for the Warm Home Discount (£150 for low-income households) or the Winter Fuel Payment (up to £300 for pensioners).
- **Water.** A free water meter can cut the bill for a smaller household.
- **Broadband and phone.** Switching, MoneyHelper says, can "save yourself hundreds of pounds on your bills". Look at the total cost including line rental, and haggle.
- **Council Tax.** Check your band is right and whether you qualify for a reduction or discount.
- **Insurance and transport.** Shop around for car insurance; book trains in advance.
- **Late fees.** "Pay your bills on time"; Direct Debit is simplest.

The other leaks are less official but just as real: forgotten subscriptions, the second car that barely moves, "loyal customer" tariffs that punish loyalty. One evening with a bank statement and a highlighter usually finds two or three.

## The mindset that makes it stick

Living well on less is mostly attention, not deprivation: know your monthly number, check once a year that you are claiming what you should, and treat every renewal letter as an invitation to shop around. None of it is clever. All of it compounds.

## This week

1. Fill in the MoneyHelper Budget Planner (or an AARP tool) with real statements and write down the monthly figure.
2. Run the Age UK benefits calculator or ring the Advice Line (UK); in the US, check SNAP and Medicare Savings Programs with your state.
3. Over State Pension age with a weekly income anywhere near £238 (single) or £363.25 (couple)? Apply for Pension Credit.
4. Take your biggest bill and spend one evening finding out whether it can be cheaper.
5. Add the annual ambushes to the monthly budget.
