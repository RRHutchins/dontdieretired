The picture most of us carry of the typical founder — young, caffeinated, fearless and broke — is a poor description of most people who start businesses, and a terrible reason not to start one yourself.

If you are thinking about a business in your fifties or sixties, you bring things the 26-year-old does not have. The job is to use them, and to avoid the one mistake experience does not protect you from: spending money before anyone has proved they will pay you.

This is general information, not financial or legal advice; an accountant or regulated adviser can look at your own situation.

## What is genuinely in your favour

**Experience.** You have watched organisations work and not work for thirty years. You know what a bad supplier looks like, what a realistic timeline is, and how to tell a good idea from a good pitch. Younger founders learn this expensively. You already paid.

**A network.** The most valuable asset most people over 50 own is the list of people who already trust them. Your first customers, referrals and honest feedback are almost always on that list.

**Capital — sometimes.** Savings, a redundancy payment or a paid-off mortgage can mean a business does not have to make money in month one. That is an enormous advantage and also the biggest risk, because it makes it possible to spend on things that do not matter. Treat capital as a runway for testing, not a budget for launching.

**Patience.** You are not trying to be enormous by Christmas. A business that pays you a fair day rate three days a week, built steadily over two years, is a success.

## What is genuinely different

Your time horizon is shorter, so a five-year plan to profitability is a poor fit. Your energy matters more, so the business should run at the pace you actually have. And the opportunity cost is real: a year on a business that fails is a year of salary and pension contributions not earned, which matters more at 58 than at 28.

None of that argues against starting. It argues for starting small and finding out fast.

## Test before you spend

The single most useful habit is to sell something before you build anything. Not a survey, not a "would you buy this?" conversation — an actual exchange of money for the smallest possible version of the thing.

If it is a service, sell ten hours to three people. If it is a product, make a small batch by hand and sell it at a market. If it is a course, run one in a hired room. What ten real customers ask, complain about and come back for is worth more than any business plan.

A few rules keep this cheap:

- **No premises, no branding, no stock beyond a first batch** until customers have paid.
- **Charge from the start.** Free trials tell you whether people like something, not whether they will pay.
- **Ask for the second order.** A repeat customer is the first real evidence you have a business.
- **Write down what surprises you.** The surprises are the business plan.

## Registering, in general terms

The formalities are simpler than people fear, and in both countries you can start trading before the paperwork.

**UK.** The simplest structure is the sole trader, which Gov.uk calls "the simplest business structure to set up and keep records for." You must register for Self Assessment with HMRC (the UK tax authority) if you "earn more than £1,000 in a tax year" from the business, and you can register voluntarily before that. From the day you start trading you must keep records, and you file a tax return each year; HMRC works out your Income Tax and National Insurance from your profits. If you are past State Pension age, you stop paying Class 4 National Insurance on self-employed profits from the start of the following tax year. A limited company is the other common route — more separation, more administration — and an accountant can help you weigh it up.

**US.** The IRS's guide to choosing a business structure describes the sole proprietorship as a business run by one person with "unlimited personal liability", filing self-employment and personal tax returns. A limited liability company (LLC) can have "one or more people" as owners who "are not personally liable", with some flexibility in how it is taxed. Requirements vary by state, and the IRS suggests consulting "a business tax specialist to confirm your specific business needs."

In both countries the order is the same: test first, register when you are trading for real, get an accountant when money arrives regularly.

## The first 90 days

**Days 1–30: find the customer.** Talk to twenty people who have the problem you solve. Sell the first version to at least one of them. No website; a phone, an email address and your network.

**Days 31–60: deliver and learn.** Serve the first customers well. Ask each what they would change, what they would pay, and who else they know. Adjust the offer. Register if it has become a real business.

**Days 61–90: repeat and decide.** Get to a handful of paying customers with at least one repeat order or referral. Then decide with real numbers: carry on, change something, or stop with your capital and your dignity intact. That last outcome is not failure; it is what testing is for.
