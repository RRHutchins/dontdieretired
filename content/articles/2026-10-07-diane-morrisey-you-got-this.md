In 2017 Diane Morrisey, a mother of six in Trumbull, Connecticut, posted a photograph of a birthday cake she had made for her daughter Frances. She had joined Instagram for one reason, which was to keep an eye on what her children were doing there.

> "My kids were like, 'Mom, you got so many likes!' I'm like, 'What's a like?'"
> — Diane Morrisey, to AARP

She kept posting what she cooked. By March 2025, when AARP profiled her, 1.4 million people were following along, and Simon & Schuster was about to publish her first cookbook, You Got This! Recipes Anyone Can Make and Everyone Will Love. She was 58, which makes her about 50 when the cake went up.

## Not a born cook

Morrisey grew up in Monroe, Connecticut, around good food, made by her mother and by a grandmother who came over from Italy. Eating it did not teach her to make it.

> "When I got married 30 years ago, I really didn't know how to cook."
> — Diane Morrisey, to AARP

She taught herself, in a household that grew to eight. She had studied art at college and taught it in an elementary (primary) school. She has also worked as a caterer and at the grocery chain Whole Foods, where, her publisher says, she oversaw the prepared-foods business for stores in New York and Connecticut.

The work taught her to present food attractively, she told AARP, so the jump to photographing dinner for Instagram "was not a big one".

What her followers came for was something else. Her posts sign off with the same three words, the ones on the cover of the book. According to a profile by Moffly Media, the phrase comes from her family's background in coaching. The book holds more than 100 recipes, among them a sheet-pan jambalaya, a chicken and barley soup with lemon and dill, and strawberry-oat bars. Her publisher describes them as simple recipes for busy home cooks.

> "Cooking is 90 percent confidence and 10 percent being able to read a recipe. You learn by getting in the kitchen. You can't be afraid."
> — Diane Morrisey, to AARP

## Why this matters

There is reasonable evidence that the habit Morrisey is selling is worth having.

In 2017 the International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity published an analysis of 11,396 British adults aged 29 to 64, all taking part in a health project called the Fenland Study. People who ate home-cooked main meals more than five times a week ate about 62 g more fruit and 98 g more vegetables a day than those who did so fewer than three times a week. That is about two extra portions by the UK's 80 g measure. They were also 28 per cent less likely to be overweight and 24 per cent less likely to carry excess body fat.

The study was a snapshot, and the authors said so: it cannot show which way cause runs. People with more time, money or interest in food may simply cook more.

What it supports is modest and useful: people who cook most of their own dinners tend to eat more plants.

## Borrowing her method

- **Repeat before you range.** Pick a short list of dishes and cook each until you stop reading the recipe.
- **Follow it once, then change one thing.** That is how a recipe becomes yours.
- **Start with a tray.** A sheet-pan dinner (a traybake, in Britain) needs one pan and one oven temperature.
- **Finish with something fresh.** Morrisey calls herbs the easiest way to lift a meal. Chop parsley or dill over the top.
- **Mind the translation.** American recipes use cups and Fahrenheit, British ones grams and Celsius. Digital scales settle it.
- **Feed someone.** She began with one cake for one daughter.
