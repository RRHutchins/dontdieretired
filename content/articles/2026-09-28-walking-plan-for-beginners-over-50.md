Walking gets a bad press from people who sell exercise. And yet it is the activity most people over 50 can begin today, keep up for decades, and build everything else on top of.

This is a plan for someone starting from very little. If you already walk the dog for an hour a day, skip to the pace and hills sections. If you are starting from nothing, our [complete starting guide](/move/how-to-get-active-after-50/) sets the scene.

## Does walking really count?

Yes, at the right pace. The NHS (the UK health service) is explicit that a daily ten-minute brisk walk "counts towards your 150 minutes of weekly exercise". The UK guideline for people over 65 is to "aim to accumulate 150 minutes... building up gradually", and this plan is built around those two words: accumulate and gradually.

The catch is the word brisk. The NHS puts it at "about 3 miles an hour" (roughly 5 km/h), "which is faster than a stroll". You do not need a watch to check. Use the talking test: at a brisk pace "you can still talk but cannot sing the words to a song".

## The plan

Rest days are part of the plan, not a failure of it. If a week feels too much, repeat it before moving on. If it feels easy, do not skip ahead; add a hill instead.

| Week | Walk length | Days per week | Pace | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 10 min | 5 | Comfortable | Out for five minutes, turn round. Just build the habit. |
| 2 | 12 min | 5 | Comfortable, brisk for the middle 4 min | Try the talking test once each walk. |
| 3 | 15 min | 5 | Brisk for 8 min | Find a route you like; boredom kills plans. |
| 4 | 18 min | 5 | Brisk for 12 min | Add one gentle slope if there is one nearby. |
| 5 | 20 min | 5 | Mostly brisk | Weekly total tops 100 minutes. Notice how normal it feels. |
| 6 | 25 min | 5 | Brisk | Two walks this week on a hillier route. |
| 7 | 30 min | 5 | Brisk | You are now at 150 minutes a week. |
| 8 | 30–35 min | 5–6 | Brisk, with hills | One longer weekend walk of 40+ min if you fancy it. |

Five walks of 30 minutes is 150 minutes: the weekly target in the UK, WHO and US guidelines. That is the finish line for this plan.

## Footwear

You need less than the shops would like you to think. The NHS advice is that "any shoes or trainers that are comfortable, provide adequate support and do not cause blisters will do", with proper walking shoes worth considering for longer walks.

What matters in practice: grip (smooth soles on wet leaves or a frosty pavement — sidewalk — are how walks end in A&E, the ER); room at the toes, since feet spread as you walk; cushioning that has not been worn flat by a decade of lawn-mowing; and socks that are not cotton, which holds sweat and causes blisters. Break new shoes in over a few short walks before a long one.

## Pace, and why you should not chase it

Brisk is a feeling — warmer, slightly breathless, still able to chat — not a number. As you get fitter the same feeling will happen at a faster pace without you trying. Resist the urge to power-walk from week two: your heart and lungs adapt quickly, but tendons, feet and knees adapt more slowly, and they are what get hurt when the pace jumps. Let speed arrive on its own.

If you like data, the NHS's free Active 10 app "tells you when you're walking fast enough".

## Hills

Hills are the cheapest upgrade in exercise: a slope strengthens your legs and pushes your breathing up without you moving faster. Add them from week four. Walk up at whatever pace keeps you in the talk-not-sing zone. Coming down, shorten your stride and keep your knees soft. If you live somewhere flat, stairs, a car park (parking garage) ramp or a footbridge all do the job.

## Walking with other people

The single best predictor of whether you will still be walking in six months is whether someone is expecting you.

In England, [Ramblers Wellbeing Walks](https://www.ramblers.org.uk/go-walking/wellbeing-walks) are "free, short walks" led by "experienced volunteers", with "no need to book or plan a route". The shortest are around ten minutes, which fits week one of this plan exactly, and "everyone's welcome", including people using wheelchairs. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own health-walk schemes; your GP surgery or library will know.

In the US, AARP's [Create the Good guide](https://createthegood.aarp.org/volunteer-guides/start-walking-group.html) suggests partnering with "senior centers, YMCAs, community centers" to find or start a group, and recruiting through "libraries, gyms, and places of worship". Their starting suggestion is the same as ours: 30-minute walks, several times a week, with "family, friends, co-workers, and neighbors". If there is no group nearby, the guide walks you through starting one.

## After week eight

Keep the five 30-minute walks and add two short strength sessions a week, which every guideline recommends and which walking alone does not provide. Our [strength training guide](/move/strength-training-after-50-why-and-how/) shows you how, at home, with no kit.

## This week

1. Walk ten minutes today, and on four other days this week. That is Week 1 done.
2. Test the talking pace once on each walk.
3. Find one walking group near you and note when it meets.
4. Check your shoes for grip and toe room before Week 3.
