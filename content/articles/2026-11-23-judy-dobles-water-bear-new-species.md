The moss came from a log at Cumming Nature Center in Naples, New York. Judy Dobles collected it and put it under a microscope. Among the usual microbes was a tardigrade that did not look like any she had seen.

"Wow, I think this might be a new species," she said, according to the Rochester Museum & Science Center, which runs the nature center.

She was right. Dobles, a retiree from Kodak's finance department and a longtime volunteer at the center, had found an animal no one had described before. It now has a name, *Mesocrista ojagwaji*.

## Two years in the moss

Tardigrades, also called water bears or moss piglets, are eight-legged animals. Most are well under a millimeter long, and the largest only just over one, less than a twentieth of an inch. They live in moss, lichen and soil, and are famous for surviving being frozen, boiled or dried out for years.

Dobles came to them by way of other citizen science: monitoring monarch butterflies, tracking invasive species and identifying wildlife on iNaturalist. She then trained specially in tardigrades with the Eagle Hill Institute in Maine. Over two years she sampled more than 143 sites on the nature center's land.

Before her survey, 12 tardigrade species had been recorded in New York State. There are now 27. Most of the new records are species already known elsewhere, found in the state for the first time. One was new to science altogether, confirmed by genetic and physical analysis by Dr. Piotr Gasiorek, a tardigrade specialist at Jagiellonian University in Poland.

## A name from the place

People are always asking her why she did not name it after herself, she told WXXI News. She chose a name tied to the land instead. The nature center stands on the ancestral homeland of the Onöndowa'ga:, or Seneca, people, and she worked with Jamie Jacobs, of the Tonawanda Seneca Nation's Turtle Clan, who has dedicated his life to preserving the language and came up with the name. *Ojagwaji* refers to the spotted, granular markings on the animal's surface.

"For me, it's more fun to partner with other people and involve them in the process," she said.

## Why this matters

About 1,549 tardigrade species are known worldwide, Dobles said. She told WXXI that about 2 million species of all kinds have been named on Earth, and that there could be 8 million. "In the soil, and at the microscopic level, there's so much we don't know," she said.

That gap is where volunteers make a difference. Professional scientists cannot sample every log in every state; someone who walks the same trails every week can. The nature center's director, Sarah Jacoby Murphy, put it simply: "Judy inspires all of us to do science exactly where we are."

## How to find your first water bear

You need moss, water and patience. The Minnesota Department of Natural Resources' guide, written by Brett Ortler for its Conservation Volunteer magazine, suggests a palm-sized piece of moss or lichen, soaked in about 20 mL of distilled water in a shallow dish for four to eight hours. Look first with a stereo microscope at about 15x, watching for movement, then at 30x: a tardigrade has eight legs with obvious claws. Wash your hands afterwards.

Tim Pearce, of the Carnegie Museum of Natural History in Pittsburgh, shook some moss from his backyard in water and found a water bear within minutes.

The Quekett Microscopical Club, a UK club for amateur and professional microscopists, says stereo microscopes magnify from about 6x to 60x, which is the right range for a first look. In the US, Cumming Nature Center is at 6472 Gulick Road, Naples; check its hours and admission page before you go.
