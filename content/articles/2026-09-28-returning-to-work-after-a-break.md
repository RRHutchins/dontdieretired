Career breaks happen for good reasons: caring for a parent or partner, a redundancy that took longer to recover from than expected, illness, or a retirement that turned out not to be what you wanted. Whatever the reason, walking back into a job market that has changed can feel like arriving at a party that started without you.

It is more manageable than it looks, and there is more help than most people know about.

## Returner programmes exist — and they are for you

Employers have built formal routes for exactly this situation. The UK government's guidance for employers, written with Women Returners and Timewise, defines returner programmes as "programmes targeted at people returning to work after a long break (typically 2 years or more)", for people "with existing work experience who have taken an extended career break for caring or other reasons."

They come in two forms. A **returnship** is a fixed-term paid contract, typically three to six months, with "a strong likelihood, but no guarantee, of an ongoing role at the end." **Supported hiring** is a permanent job with extra help built in. Both are meant to offer competitive pay and "additional support such as coaching, training and mentoring."

Search for "returner programme" or "returnship" with your field, and check the careers pages of large employers you would consider. They are not age-limited; the qualifying feature is the break, not your birthday.

## Update the CV — or the résumé

In the UK it is a CV; in the US a résumé. A returner's document has one particular problem: the reader's eye goes to the dates, sees a gap, and stops. So change what they see first.

Open with a short profile — three or four lines on what you do and what you are looking for now. Follow it with an achievements section: the budgets you ran, the teams you led, the systems you know. Only then the chronological history.

Include the break as a line in the history, briefly and without apology: "2021–2025: Career break (family care)". Anything relevant you did during it — volunteering, a committee, a course — goes in as experience, because it is.

Keep it to two pages, cut jobs from more than twenty years ago to a single line each, and leave out your date of birth: Gov.uk notes that when you apply for a job "you do not have to give your date of birth".

## Handle the gap honestly

Interviewers will ask about the break. The worst answers are the evasive ones. The best is one plain sentence you have practised until it sounds like you: what you did, that it is finished or managed, and that you are ready. Suppose you spent four years caring for a parent; the sentence might be: "I took four years out to care for my mother. That period has ended, I've spent the last few months getting my skills current, and I'm ready to get back to work." Then stop talking. Most interviewers will move on; the ones who do not are telling you something about the employer. Do not over-explain, do not apologise, and do not pretend the break was something else. Honesty is also the most convincing thing.

## Practise the interview

If you have not been interviewed for fifteen years, your fluency has faded. It comes back in a fortnight. Write down six stories from your working life — a problem you solved, a person you developed, a mistake you recovered from — each with a situation, what you did and what happened. Most interview questions are an invitation to tell one of them. Practise out loud with someone who will ask follow-ups, at least three times. Video interviews are common now: look at the camera, not the screen.

## Upskilling: the free routes

A short course fills the most obvious gap and gives the CV a recent date that is not a break.

**UK (England).** Skills Bootcamps are, in the words of the Gov.uk JobHelp page, free, flexible courses lasting "up to 16 weeks" that teach "specific skills employers in different sectors are looking for", and at the end "you will be offered a job interview with an employer." They are open to people aged over 19 in England who are "employed or self-employed, or currently unemployed", and can be classroom-based, on the job, online or a mix. The Department for Education's Education Hub lists three further free routes: **Free Courses for Jobs**, which funds training that helps you "learn new skills or apply for work"; **Multiply**, "free courses to improve your maths skills"; and **essential skills** courses in English, maths and digital skills "for everyday life". The National Careers Service (0800 100 900) can tell you what is running near you. Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland have their own schemes.

**US.** Community colleges are the workhorse of adult retraining, with short certificate courses often run in the evenings at low cost. AARP Foundation's **BACK TO WORK 50+** is, in AARP's words, a "free program that offers career coaching and job search workshops to help people 50 and older find work"; AARP says it has helped more than 100,000 people, with in-person and online sessions and a toll-free number on the AARP Foundation website. AARP's job-search resources page also points to Career One-Stop Centers, which provide "employment assistance to job seekers in over 2,000 communities", and to job boards aimed at older workers.

Rewrite the top of the CV, write your one sentence about the break, book a practice interview with a friend, and enquire about one course. The return is a project, not a leap, and projects get done a task at a time.
