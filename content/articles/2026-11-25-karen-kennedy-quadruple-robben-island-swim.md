At the end of each crossing, Karen Kennedy walked out of the Atlantic, turned around and walked back in, until she had swum between the mainland at Blouberg and Robben Island, off Cape Town, four times. In all she swam nearly 30 km (about 18.5 miles) in cold water, in "skins": a swimsuit, with no wetsuit. It took just over 10 hours.

Kennedy, 59, a swimmer and coach in Cape Town, is the first woman to complete a quadruple Robben Island crossing in skins, and the fastest, according to reports in the Weekend Argus and on Eyewitness News in April. Both said the record was pending ratification by Guinness World Records. The swim also had to follow the rules of the Cape Long Distance Swimming Association.

> "The decision was sparked after my Triple RI (Robben Island) crossing in skins in 2025."
> — Karen Kennedy, to Eyewitness News

That triple, three crossings in a row, had made her the second woman to complete one in skins, Eyewitness News reported, and the fastest.

## The third crossing

The format is simple and unforgiving: four consecutive crossings between the mainland and the island, leaving the water briefly at each end before going back in. Her swimming partner, Graham du Toit, swam beside her. A support boat, paid for jointly by du Toit and the South African Rooibos Council, guided them, kept them safe and handed over food and drink on a schedule.

> "I felt most tested on the third crossing, about 3km from Robben Island (20km in)."
> — Karen Kennedy, to Eyewitness News

The current there was very strong and the water very choppy. She swallowed seawater, she was tired and her legs cramped. She told Eyewitness News that when the urge to stop comes, she tells herself to relax, and that "You've got this."

She broke the distance down. She thought in 30-minute windows between feeds of carbohydrate, protein, electrolytes and warm rooibos tea with honey. "One crossing at a time," she told the Weekend Argus. "One stroke at a time."

She is clear that it was not a solo effort. A marathon swimmer, she said, has a spouse or partner, trainers, a coach, swim buddies and an official support team on the day. "As far as I'm concerned, this makes it a team sport."

## Twenty years out of the water

Kennedy was a provincial swimmer when she was young. Then she stepped away from the sport for more than two decades, a time the Weekend Argus describes as inactive. She came back to the water in her early forties and took up cold-water ocean swimming after moving to Cape Town. Since then, the paper lists, she has finished on the podium at South Africa's Midmar Mile several times and won silver in the 3 km open water race at the World Masters Games.

## Why this matters

Cold water is the thing to respect. The RNLI, the UK and Ireland's lifeboat charity, says that getting into water under 15°C (59°F) can seriously affect your ability to breathe and move, and that the average sea temperature around the UK and Ireland is just 12°C (54°F). Its advice is to go in slowly, never jump or dive, splash water on your neck and face, and wait for the shock to pass. If you get into trouble, it says, float: tilt your head back with your ears under, relax and breathe, then call for help or swim to safety.

It also says to swim with a buddy, wear a wetsuit if you want extra warmth and buoyancy, tell someone on shore where you are going and when you will be back, and talk to a health professional before your first cold swim. In an emergency at the coast, call 999 or 112 in the UK and ask for the coastguard; in the US, call 911.

## How to start

The easiest way in is a group. Our page on [joining a sea swimming group](/list/join-a-sea-swimming-group/) lists free meet-ups and clubs in the UK and the US, and the RNLI suggests a local group for beginners because you look out for each other. Start with short, slow dips in company. If you already swim in open water, borrow Kennedy's method: break a long swim into 30-minute blocks with a feed after each.
