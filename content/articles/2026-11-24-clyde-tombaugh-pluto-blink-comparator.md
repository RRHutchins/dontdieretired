At about 4 o'clock on the afternoon of Tuesday, February 18, 1930, in an office at Lowell Observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona, Clyde Tombaugh saw a faint dot jump. He had been at the eyepiece since about 9 that morning, flipping between two photographs of the same patch of sky in the constellation Gemini, taken a few nights apart in January.

The stars on the two plates stayed exactly where they were. One speck did not. After about 45 minutes of measuring and checking it against backup plates, the observatory's history says, he went to tell his colleagues. His words, as the history gives them: "I have found your Planet X."

## A planet on paper

Planet X was the idea of Percival Lowell, who founded the observatory. He first proposed an unseen ninth planet in 1902, Lowell Observatory says, and searched for it from 1905 until his death in 1916. A legal fight with his widow then held up the work, the American Physical Society (APS) says.

In 1927 the observatory acquired a new telescope for the search, the APS says. Abbott Lawrence Lowell, the president of Harvard, gave $10,000 for it, according to the observatory. Alvan Clark & Sons made it from 13-inch (33 cm) glass discs, and its dome went up in 1928, the observatory's archive says.

## The young man from the farm

Tombaugh was born in Illinois in 1906 and grew up on a farm in Kansas. As a teenager he looked through his uncle's three-inch telescope, the APS says, and at 20 he began building his own. By 1928 his third backyard telescope was good enough for him to draw Mars and Jupiter. He sent the drawings to Vesto Slipher, the director at Lowell, who offered him a job. Tombaugh arrived in Flagstaff on January 15, 1929. He was 22 and had no degree.

## How the blink comparator works

Tombaugh photographed each region of sky on glass plates 14 by 17 inches (36 by 43 cm), then photographed it again a few nights later. By day he put the pair in a blink comparator, a Zeiss instrument that the Smithsonian's National Air and Space Museum describes as a special kind of microscope. Turning a small dial flipped a mirror so that he saw one plate, then the other, in the same place.

Distant stars do not move in a few days; asteroids move a lot; something beyond Neptune would move a little. On February 18 the speck he found had shifted about 3 mm (an eighth of an inch) between the exposures, the APS says: too slowly for an asteroid.

The sources differ on which plates he was comparing. The observatory's archive says plates were taken on January 21, 23 and 29; the Smithsonian names the pair of January 23 and 29; the Science Museum in London says only that they were taken a few nights apart that January.

## A birthday announcement

The observatory spent more than three weeks checking before it announced anything. The news went out by telegram on the evening of March 12, and Harvard College Observatory announced it on March 13, 1930, which would have been Percival Lowell's 75th birthday. It was also the anniversary of William Herschel's discovery of Uranus in 1781. Lowell's own history page says Slipher sent the telegram; its archive exhibition says it was Roger Putnam, Lowell's nephew and the observatory's trustee.

## A name from the breakfast table

On the morning of March 14, in Oxford, England, Falconer Madan, the retired head of the Bodleian Library, read the news to his 11-year-old granddaughter, Venetia Burney. She knew her myths, and Pluto, the Roman god of the underworld, was a name not yet used. "Why not call it Pluto?" she said, as she recalled in a 2006 interview with NASA.

Madan sent the idea to the Oxford astronomer Herbert Hall Turner, who cabled it to Lowell. The Smithsonian says Lowell's astronomers chose it in a vote in May 1930, ahead of names such as Minerva, which already belonged to an asteroid; the APS says the planet was named later in March. The APS gives a second reason it appealed: its first two letters are Percival Lowell's initials.

## Not the planet they were looking for

Pluto was not Planet X. The Science Museum notes that it was far too small to match Lowell's predictions, and later, more accurate figures for the orbits of Uranus and Neptune removed the need for a Planet X at all. Its mass, measured only after its moon Charon was found in 1978, is about 0.002 of the Earth's, the APS says. In 2006 the International Astronomical Union reclassified it as a dwarf planet. The prediction was wrong; the search found something real anyway.

## What happened next

Tombaugh went on to the University of Kansas on a scholarship, found hundreds of asteroids and, by the observatory's estimate, spent some 7,000 hours at the blink comparator. He died in 1997. Some of his ashes travelled to Pluto on NASA's New Horizons spacecraft, which flew past it in 2015 carrying a dust counter named after Venetia Burney. She became an accountant and a teacher of economics and math, and died in 2009, aged 90.

The telescope is back in its original dome at Lowell, where visitors can see it.
