You can start Social Security retirement benefits at any age from 62 to 70. Start early and the monthly check is smaller for life; wait and it grows, up to 70. The right month depends on your health, your work, your savings and, if you are married, your spouse. Nobody can pick it for you from a chart, and this guide will not try.

This is general information, not financial advice. A fee-only financial adviser, or a free nonprofit counselor, can look at your own situation, and SSA's staff can explain your record (1-800-772-1213).

## Three ages that matter

**62** is the earliest you can claim retirement benefits.

**Full retirement age** is when you get 100% of your benefit. For anyone born in 1960 or later it is [67](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/1960.html). For people born from 1955 to 1959 it steps up two months a year, from 66 and 2 months to 66 and 10 months ([SSA's chart](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/agereduction.html)).

**70** is when the increase for waiting stops. SSA's [delayed retirement credits](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/delayret.html) add 8% for each year you wait past full retirement age (two-thirds of 1% a month) for anyone born in 1943 or later. There is no gain from waiting beyond 70.

## What each age pays

SSA's own figures for someone with a full retirement age of 67:

| If you start at | Your own benefit, as a share of your full benefit | A spouse's benefit, as a share of the worker's full benefit |
|---|---|---|
| 62 | 70% | 32.5% |
| 67 (full retirement age) | 100% | 50% |
| 70 | 124% | 50% (no increase for waiting) |

Sources: SSA's [reduction chart](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/agereduction.html), its [born-1960 page](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/1960.html) and its [delayed retirement table](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/1960-delay.html), which notes its percentages "are estimates due to rounding."

SSA's own worked example: a $1,000 monthly benefit at full retirement age becomes $700 if started at 62, and a $500 spouse's benefit becomes $325.

The ages in between move month by month. At 64 you would get 80%; at 68, 108%; at 69, 116%. Every month you wait from 62 to 70 raises the check a little.

All three amounts then rise with the yearly cost-of-living adjustment. For 2027 that is [3.6%](https://www.ssa.gov/cola/factsheets/2027.html).

## The trade-off in plain words

Claiming early gives you more checks but smaller ones. Waiting gives you fewer checks but larger ones, for the rest of your life. Which works out better depends partly on how long you live, which nobody knows.

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, a US government agency, does not name a best age. Its [claiming guide](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/consumer-tools/retirement/before-you-claim/) asks five questions instead:

- "Are you married?"
- "Do you plan to continue working in your 60s?"
- "Will your expenses decrease after you retire?"
- "Do you expect to have additional sources of retirement income beyond Social Security?"
- "Do you expect to live a long life?"

It also points out that more years of work can raise your benefit by replacing low-earning years in your record.

## Spousal benefits

A husband or wife can get a benefit on the other's work record. According to the [Social Security Blog](https://www.ssa.gov/blog/en/posts/2024-07-11.html):

- The most a spouse can get is 50% of the worker's benefit at full retirement age.
- The worker must already be getting retirement or disability benefits.
- The spouse must be at least 62, or any age if caring for a child under 16 or a disabled child entitled on the worker's record.
- Claimed before the spouse's own full retirement age, the spousal benefit is permanently reduced. At 62, with a full retirement age of 67, it is 32.5% of the worker's full benefit, not 50% ([SSA's chart](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/1960.html)).
- **The worker's delayed retirement credits do not raise the spousal benefit.** Waiting past full retirement age helps the worker's own check, not the spouse's.
- If you have your own benefit too, you apply for both and get the higher of the two, not both added together. SSA's example: someone with a $1,000 benefit of her own gets an extra $250 as a spouse, $1,250 in total, because her own benefit is less than half her spouse's full-age amount.

A divorced spouse may claim on a former spouse's record if the marriage lasted at least 10 years.

## Survivor benefits

This is where the higher earner's choice matters most. A surviving spouse can get up to 100% of the deceased's benefit at full retirement age for survivors, which is between 66 and 67, says SSA's [survivor amounts page](https://ssa.gov/survivor/amount). "Payments start at 71.5% of your spouse's benefit and increase the longer you wait to apply." And the delayed credits a worker earns *do* carry through to a surviving spouse's benefit, [SSA says](https://www.ssa.gov/blog/en/posts/2024-07-11.html).

Who can claim, from SSA's [eligibility page](https://www.ssa.gov/survivor/eligibility) and [surviving spouses post](https://www.ssa.gov/blog/en/posts/2026-10-29.html):

- From 60, or 50 to 59 with a disability, or at any age if caring for the deceased's child under 16 or disabled before 22.
- Not if you remarried before 60 (50 with a disability).
- A divorced spouse married for at least 10 years may qualify.

If you also have your own retirement benefit, the two are not added together. You can take one and switch later: SSA's example is starting on survivor benefits "and then change to Retirement at age 70 when that payment is highest." You cannot apply for survivor benefits online; call 1-800-772-1213.

## Working while you claim: the earnings test

If you claim before full retirement age and keep working, SSA may hold back some benefits. The limits come from SSA's [2027 COLA fact sheet](https://www.ssa.gov/cola/factsheets/2027.html):

| | 2026 | 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Under full retirement age all year: $1 withheld for every $2 above | $24,480 ($2,040 a month) | $25,560 ($2,130 a month) |
| The year you reach full retirement age: $1 withheld for every $3 above, counting only earnings before that month | $65,160 ($5,430 a month) | $67,920 ($5,660 a month) |

From the month you reach full retirement age, there is no limit.

Only wages and net self-employment earnings count. Pensions, annuities, investment income, interest and capital gains do not, says SSA's booklet [How Work Affects Your Benefits](https://www.ssa.gov/pubs/EN-05-10069.pdf). In your first year of retirement a special rule pays a full check for any month your earnings stay under the monthly limit.

SSA's own example: someone who claims at 62 in January 2026 with a $600 monthly benefit and earns $26,080 is $1,600 over the limit, so $800 is withheld. SSA stops the January and February checks, pays $600 from March, and pays the $400 balance in 2027.

**Withheld money is not lost.** In SSA's words, "The amount that your benefits are reduced, however, isn't lost." Your benefit is recalculated upward at full retirement age. SSA's example: a $910 benefit at 62, with 12 months withheld, becomes $975 a month at 67 (in today's dollars).

## Taxes and Medicare

Benefits can be taxed. [SSA says](https://ssa.gov/faqs/en/questions/KA-02471.html) you may pay federal income tax on up to 85% of them if your "combined income" (adjusted gross income, plus tax-exempt interest, plus half your benefits) is above $25,000 filing alone or $32,000 filing jointly.

Medicare is separate from claiming. If you delay Social Security, SSA [advises](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/delayret.html) signing up for Medicare at 65 anyway, or your coverage may start later and cost more. Our [Medicare guide](/money/medicare-explained/) has the dates.

## Run your own numbers

Your [my Social Security account](https://www.ssa.gov/myaccount/index.html) shows your Statement, your earnings history and estimates "you could receive at different ages." Its retirement estimate compares [62, full retirement age and 70](https://www.ssa.gov/OACT/anypia/index.html) and lets you enter income you expect to earn. Our guide to [reading your Social Security statement](/money/check-your-state-pension-forecast-or-social-security-statement/) explains the figures.

## This week

1. Sign in to my Social Security and write down your three estimates: 62, full retirement age and 70.
2. Check your earnings history line by line.
3. If you are married, compare both records, and ask what the higher earner's choice would mean for a survivor.
4. Working before full retirement age? Set your expected 2027 pay against $25,560.
5. Take your answers to the CFPB's five questions to a fee-only adviser or to SSA before you apply.

> **If you live in the UK**
> The State Pension (the UK's government pension; Social Security in the US) works differently. If you reach State Pension age on or after 6 April 2016, putting off your claim raises your weekly pension by 1% for every 9 weeks you wait, "just under 5.8%" for every 52 weeks, and you must defer for at least 9 weeks, says [GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/deferring-state-pension/if-you-reach-state-pension-age-on-or-after-6-april-2016). You do not need to do anything to defer: if you do not claim, it is [deferred automatically](https://www.gov.uk/deferring-state-pension), though you cannot build it up while you or your partner get certain benefits, and any extra payments could be taxed. Before deciding, [check your State Pension forecast](https://www.gov.uk/check-state-pension) to see how much you could get, when, and whether you can increase it.
