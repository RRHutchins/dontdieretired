On the evening of December 17, 1843, the artist John Callcott Horsley called at the home of his friend Henry Cole with a drawing. Cole wrote it down in his diary that night: "In the Evg Horsley came & brought his design for Christmas Cards."

Cole had a problem that many people will recognize in late November. Christmas was busy in the Cole household, the Victoria and Albert Museum (V&A) says, and his unanswered letters were piling up. He wanted a way to send good wishes to everyone at once. Horsley's drawing was the answer, and the museums that hold copies of it call it the first commercial Christmas card.

## The man with the post

Cole, who lived from 1808 to 1882, was a civil servant with a habit of starting things. The V&A, which he went on to found and run as its first director, calls him an educator and inventor as well. The Library of Congress adds that he wrote children's books and helped develop Britain's railways.

He also had a hand in the reform that made the card possible. The Postal Museum in London says Cole worked as an assistant to Rowland Hill on the introduction of the Penny Post, the flat, cheap postage rate that made letters affordable in the 1840s. The V&A says the new post was already encouraging people to send seasonal greetings on decorated letter paper and visiting cards. Cole's idea was to print the greeting itself.

## What the card showed

Horsley's design has three panels. In the middle, three generations of a family sit at a table and raise a toast. On either side, in black and white, are two acts of charity, which The Postal Museum names as "feeding the hungry" and "clothing the naked". The message reads "A Merry Christmas and a Happy New Year to You", with blank lines for the names of the person receiving it and the person sending it, the Library of Congress says.

On Cole's own copy, now at the V&A, Horsley drew a tiny self-portrait in the bottom right corner in place of a signature, with the date "Xmasse, 1843".

## A thousand cards and a glass of wine

Both the V&A and The Postal Museum say 1,000 cards were printed. Cole used what he needed and sold the rest for a shilling each, The Postal Museum says, under the pen name Felix Summerly.

The picture caused a stir. Among the family raising a toast are children with glasses of wine, and The Postal Museum says that proved controversial at a time when the temperance movement was campaigning against drink.

## Where the accounts differ

The sources agree on the year, the artist and the price. They do not agree on much else.

- **Did it sell?** The V&A says a shilling was expensive at the time and that the venture was "judged a commercial flop". The Postal Museum says the row over the wine did not stop people buying the cards and that more were printed to meet demand.
- **How was it made?** The Postal Museum says the cards were printed lithographically and then colored by hand by a professional colorer named Mason. The Library of Congress says Horsley used chromolithography, printing in several layers of color, and that thousands of copies could then be made.
- **Was it really first?** For a time, the V&A says, another card, by the artist William Maw Egley, was thought to be older, because its date was read as 1842. It is now dated 1848.

The same December saw Charles Dickens publish "A Christmas Carol", which both the V&A and the Library of Congress point out. Even so, the V&A says the habit of sending cards was not firmly established until the 1870s.

## How the card crossed the Atlantic

In America, the card took off through Louis Prang, a German immigrant printer in Boston who lived from 1824 to 1909. The Henry Ford, a museum of American history, says he began making small Christmas cards for the American market in the mid-1870s, with flowers and a short holiday message. By 1880 he was selling nearly five million a year.

That spring Prang ran a design competition with $2,000 in prizes for the top four designs. It drew more than 800 entries, shown at the American Art Gallery in New York. The next contest, in February 1881, drew nearly 1,500, and later rounds let the public vote for their favorites. He stopped the contests after 1884, The Henry Ford says, because most of the amateur entries were of little use to him. By the early 1890s his large, frilly, expensive cards were going out of fashion, and he moved on to educational materials.

## What it grew into

The V&A now holds a national collection of more than 30,000 cards for every occasion, more than half of them for Christmas. One of Cole's first cards sold at auction in 2013 for £22,000, the museum says, and about a billion Christmas cards are bought in the UK each year. The Postal Museum says very few of the 1843 cards survive.
