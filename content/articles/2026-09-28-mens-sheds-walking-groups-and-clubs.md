The friends you made at 25 came free with the job, the kids' school or the flat-share. After 50 those supply lines dry up one by one, and if you wait for friendship to arrive it generally does not. The fix is not to become more outgoing. It is to put yourself somewhere the same people gather every week and let time do what it did when you were younger. This article is the practical companion to our [guide to friendship and community after 50](/connect/friendship-and-community-after-50/).

## Why this matters more than it sounds

The US National Institute on Aging lists what social isolation and loneliness are associated with: "High blood pressure, Heart disease, Obesity, Weakened immune function, Anxiety, Depression, Cognitive decline, Dementia". Its advice is not complicated: "Join a cause and get involved in your community", and "check out resources and programs at your local social service agencies, community and senior centers, and public libraries". It also notes that "helping others through volunteering helps you feel less lonely". The groups below are the ones that make that advice easy to follow.

## Men's Sheds

The UK Men's Sheds Association describes a Shed as "similar to garden sheds – a place to pursue practical interests at leisure, to practice skills and enjoy making and mending", with the difference that "they're about social connections and friendship building, sharing skills and knowledge, and of course a lot of laughter." You do not need to be handy; someone will show you. And despite the name, "Sheds typically attract older men, but many have younger members and women too." The association's Find a Shed page lists them by location.

In the US, the US Men's Shed Association describes a Shed as "a workshop and social club where men gather to build things, learn new skills, and pass on what they know — no pressure, no agenda." Its site has both a Find a Shed page and a Start a Shed guide, in case your town does not have one yet. Starting one is a project in itself, which is rather the point.

## parkrun: run, walk or hold the stopwatch

parkrun is free and happens "every Saturday" over 5 km (about 3 miles) in parks, on beaches and along promenades. Its own line is the one to remember: "It doesn't matter how fast you go. It doesn't matter what you're wearing. What matters is taking part." Walking the whole course is normal.

The under-used way in is volunteering. "All events are organised by a group of our wonderful dedicated volunteers", which means every Saturday needs a team of people who are not running. Volunteering gives you a clear job next to the same people each week, with none of the "what do I say?" problem. Register on the parkrun site, find your nearest event, and either take part or contact the local team about helping.

## Ramblers and walking groups

The Ramblers run "up to 50,000 Ramblers group walks every year, all guided by a walk leader", through "over 500 Ramblers groups right across Britain". You find them by entering a postcode on the Ramblers site, where each listing gives the distance so you can start short. Walking is the ideal friendship activity: side by side rather than face to face, with natural pauses, and usually a pub or café at the end.

Outside Britain, look for local hiking clubs, council-run "health walks", or walking groups on Meetup (below).

## u3a, libraries and everything else

**u3a** is "a UK-wide movement of locally-run groups providing a wide range of opportunities to come together to learn for fun", open to "everyone who is not in full-time employment", with over 435,000 members in more than 1,000 local u3as. Each u3a runs dozens of interest groups; you join one u3a and can dip into any of them. If you like learning with a side of coffee, this is the one.

**Libraries** are the most under-rated social venue in any town: reading groups, knit-and-natter, local history, chess, IT help sessions, and the noticeboard that tells you what else is on. Free, warm, and you can sit in a corner until you feel brave.

**Meetup** is the website and app that "creates possibilities to find and build local communities" — groups organised around anything from board games to birdwatching. Quality varies, so favour groups that meet regularly and have recent photos of actual meetings. It is especially useful in the US, where the national club networks above are thinner on the ground.

And if you want purpose alongside company, [volunteering](/connect/volunteering-after-50-where-to-start/) delivers both.

## How to turn up the first time

The first visit is the whole obstacle, so make it as small as possible.

1. **Email or message first.** "I'd like to come along on Saturday, is there anything I need to bring?" Now someone is expecting you, and you have a name to ask for.
2. **Arrive slightly early.** Late arrivals walk into a formed group; early ones help set out the chairs.
3. **Have one question ready.** "How long have you been coming?" works everywhere. People like explaining their thing.
4. **Stay for the coffee.** The activity is the excuse; the twenty minutes afterwards is where the friendship is.
5. **Go back the following week.** Regularity is what turns "the new person" into "one of us". Give any group three visits before you judge it.

## This week

1. Choose one group from this list that meets weekly and is within easy reach.
2. Message them, then go once and stay for the coffee.
3. Put next week's session in the diary before you leave.
4. Tell someone at home what you are doing; it makes you more likely to go.
