The surfboard cost Katie Pahlow $50, second-hand, from a newspaper advertisement in Melbourne. She was 17. It went into the shed, and life happened around it for the next 43 years.

> "When I was literally 17, living in Melbourne in the western suburbs, I bought myself a surfboard in the paper second-hand for $50."
> — Katie Pahlow, ABC News

She never learned to ride it. Forty-three years later, on turning 60 in July 2023, she booked her first surf lessons on Victoria's coast. She has been surfing since.

## The "third act"

Pahlow credits Jane Fonda's idea of a "third act" — the notion that the decades after 60 are not an epilogue but a whole new part of life with its own purpose — for finally pushing her to do it. It is a useful frame. If you think of 60 as the beginning of the end, a surf lesson looks frivolous. If you think of it as the start of a thirty-year act, it looks like a sensible opening scene.

## She is not alone

The ABC's report makes clear this is a trend, not a one-off. Pam Burridge, the 1990 world surfing champion, now 60, runs a surf school at Mollymook in New South Wales and reports rising numbers of mature women booking lessons and retreats. Surf schools built for beginners — with soft boards, small waves and patient coaching — mean the barrier is far lower than it was in 1980.

## Why this story matters more than the athletic ones

We run a lot of stories about people who start late and become champions. They are inspiring, but they can also be intimidating: not everyone wants to deadlift for their country. Pahlow's story is about something else — closing a loop. Doing the thing you meant to do. There is no record at stake. The reward is the feeling of standing up on a wave that you first imagined at seventeen.

That is available to almost everyone, in some form. The board is in the shed. Book the lesson.
