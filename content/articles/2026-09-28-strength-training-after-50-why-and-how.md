Ask most people over 50 what exercise they do and the answer is walking, or the garden, or "I'm on my feet all day". All good. None of it is strength training, the one thing that directly addresses what age does to muscle. Here is why, and how to start with a chair and a wall. For the bigger picture, see our [complete starting guide](/move/how-to-get-active-after-50/).

## Sarcopenia, in plain words

Sarcopenia is the medical name for age-related muscle loss. According to the US National Institutes of Health, the process "begins at age 30" and muscle is lost at "about 3–5% every 10 years". Harvard Health gives the same figure and notes that over a lifetime "most men will lose about 30% of their muscle mass".

Three to five per cent a decade sounds gentle. By your sixties it has been compounding for thirty years, and the NIH is blunt about what it looks like: difficulty "standing from a chair, walking, opening jars, or carrying groceries". People with sarcopenia are "nearly twice as likely to be hospitalized".

Muscle loss with age is normal; it is not compulsory. The NIH's advice is that "you need to have resistance training". Harvard cites a review of 49 studies of men aged 50 to 83 doing progressive resistance training who "averaged a 2.4-pound increase in lean body mass", and quotes one of its exercise physiologists: "it is never too late to rebuild muscle and maintain it." That is why every major guideline — UK, WHO, US — asks for muscle-strengthening activity on at least two days a week, and the WHO asks people over 65 for strength and balance work "on 3 or more days a week".

## The six basic movements

Nearly everything the body does is one of six patterns. One exercise for each is a whole-body programme.

1. **Squat** — sitting down and standing up. *At home:* sit-to-stand from a chair, then mini-squats.
2. **Hinge** — bending at the hips with a flat back, as when picking something up. *At home:* hands on hips, push your bottom back, chest forward, stand tall.
3. **Push** — pressing something away. *At home:* wall press-ups, then press-ups against a worktop (counter).
4. **Pull** — drawing something towards you. *At home:* a resistance band on a door handle, or a bag of books rowed to your hip.
5. **Carry** — walking while holding weight. *At home:* two full shopping bags walked the length of the hall and back.
6. **Step** — one leg at a time. *At home:* step-ups on the bottom stair, holding the banister.

Add calf raises (up onto tiptoes, holding the worktop) for ankles. The NHS strength exercises page lists several of these; its advice is to use "a stable chair without wheels" and "build up slowly and aim to gradually increase the repetitions of each exercise over time".

## Sets and reps for beginners

A "rep" is one repetition; a "set" is a group of reps followed by a rest. Beginners overthink this, so:

- **One or two sets of 5–10 reps** of each movement. The NHS starting points are five sit-to-stands, five mini-squats, five calf raises, and for wall press-ups "3 sets of 5 to 10 repetitions".
- **Rest about a minute** between sets.
- **Move slowly.** The NHS note on sit-to-stands is "the slower the repetitions, the better".
- **Stop two reps before you couldn't do another.** Effort, not a fight.
- **Two days a week,** with a day between them. Fifteen to twenty minutes is plenty at first.

Mild soreness a day or two later is normal. Joint pain during an exercise is a reason to stop that one and ask a physiotherapist (physical therapist) or GP (family doctor). If you have a medical condition or have not exercised in years, the NHS advice is to "speak to a GP first". This is general information, not medical advice.

## Bodyweight, bands or dumbbells?

**Bodyweight** — chair, wall, stairs — is where nearly everyone should start, and it is enough for the first two or three months. **Resistance bands** are the best value in home exercise: they live in a drawer and give you a proper pulling exercise, which bodyweight struggles to. **Dumbbells** (or kettlebells) are what you graduate to when bands stop feeling like work; start lighter than you think.

No gym is needed, though one session with a qualified trainer, or a beginners' class at a leisure centre (rec center), is worth it if you want your form checked.

## How to progress

Muscle grows in response to a demand slightly bigger than last time. Every fortnight or so, change one thing:

1. **More reps** — 5, then 8, then 10, then 12.
2. **More sets** — one, then two, then three.
3. **Slower** — three seconds down, three up.
4. **Harder version** — wall press-up to worktop press-up; sit-to-stand to mini-squat.
5. **More load** — a heavier band, then a dumbbell.

Change one at a time. If a session feels much harder than the last, go back a step. Write down what you did; in six weeks you will not believe where you started.

## Strength and everything else

Strength is not instead of walking. Walking looks after your heart and lungs; strength looks after the muscle that gets you up the stairs and off the floor. Add a couple of minutes of [balance practice](/move/balance-exercises-to-prevent-falls/) and you have the whole recipe the guidelines ask for.

## This week

1. Do five slow sit-to-stands and ten wall press-ups, twice this week, a day or two apart.
2. Try the other four movements once, just to learn them.
3. Put two strength days in the diary for next week.
4. If you have a condition that worries you, ask your GP which movements to be careful with.
