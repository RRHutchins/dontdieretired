On January 15, 1885, on a farm in Jericho, Vermont, Wilson Bentley managed something nobody is known to have done before. He photographed a single snow crystal through a microscope, sharply enough to see its shape. He was 19.

The Smithsonian calls him the first person to photograph a single snowflake. Over the rest of his life he took more than 5,000 such pictures. His neighbors gave him the name that stuck: Snowflake.

## A microscope for his fifteenth birthday

Bentley was born in Jericho in 1865 and was taught by his mother, according to the Smithsonian Institution Archives. He was given a microscope for his fifteenth birthday and began looking at snow under it.

Drawing the crystals was not enough. He needed a photograph, and he needed it before the flake melted or evaporated. His answer, Smithsonian Magazine says, was a mechanism that combined a microscope with a view camera, the kind with a long folding bellows. The first success came in 1885, four years after the microscope arrived, the Archives says.

He spent most of his 66 years as a farmer, Smithsonian Magazine notes. The snow work happened in the cold, in the winters, alongside it.

## "Miracles of beauty"

Bentley was not shy about what he saw. Smithsonian Magazine reports that he called the crystals "miracles of beauty" and believed that each snowflake is "as different from its fellows as human beings are from each other."

He was generous with the pictures, too. He sold many of his glass plates to schools and colleges for 5 cents apiece, according to Smithsonian Magazine, and he never copyrighted his work. He also wrote some 60 articles, the Smithsonian says.

## The Smithsonian said no, then yes

In the early 1900s Bentley wrote to the Smithsonian, worried that his work could be lost to fire or accident, and offered it copies. The Archives says about 500 of his prints are there today.

The welcome was not wholehearted. Smithsonian Magazine says that in 1904 a curator, George Merrill, turned down a manuscript Bentley sent with nearly 20 years of photographs, calling it unscientific. Western New York Heritage says that after Bentley's death his niece offered the whole collection to the Smithsonian, and it refused. Years later the Archives' own staff found hundreds of his glass negatives in a box and reunited them with the prints.

## The book, and the blizzard

In 1931 Bentley and William J. Humphreys, a physicist at the US Weather Bureau, published *Snow Crystals*. The Smithsonian Institution Archives says the book holds 2,300 of his photographs; Smithsonian Magazine says 2,500.

He died at his Jericho farm on December 23 that year. The Archives says he caught pneumonia after walking through a blizzard.

## Where the plates went

In 1947 the Buffalo Museum of Science acquired the collection, led by Chauncey Hamlin of the Buffalo Society of Natural Sciences. Western New York Heritage puts it at more than 10,000 plates, with Bentley's weather notebooks. The museum's own collections page lists about 5,000 glass photomicrographs. The difference may be what is being counted, but neither page explains it.

## Is it true that no two are alike?

Bentley's pictures did more than anything else to make "no two snowflakes are alike" a household phrase. The Smithsonian says his images supported that belief.

Physics gives a careful answer. Kenneth Libbrecht, a Caltech physicist who studies snow, says it depends on what you mean by "alike" and by "snowflake". Tiny, simple crystals, the plain six-sided prisms, can look very much like one another under a microscope. But for large, branching stars, he concludes, it is unlikely that any two have ever looked completely alike in the whole history of the planet.

So the saying is true of the snowflakes Bentley made famous, and not quite true of every speck of ice that falls.

## Seeing it for yourself

The Jericho Historical Society keeps a free exhibit of Bentley's original photographs and his camera at the Old Red Mill in Jericho. His other collection sits in Buffalo. And the next snowfall, wherever you are, is the original.
