Somewhere is a pension from a job you barely remember. You were enrolled, you left, the paperwork went to an address you moved from twenty years ago, and the pot has sat there since. This is normal and fixable, and the searches are free. This is general information, not financial advice; a regulated adviser can look at your own situation once you know what you have.

## Start with a list, not a search

The official tracing services work best when you give them something to go on. So before you open a browser, list every employer you have had, with rough dates. MoneyHelper (the UK's government-backed guidance service) suggests including "all jobs where you may have had pension arrangements, even brief employment". Beside each, note anything you remember about a pension: a provider, a scheme name, a letter you once had.

## The UK: Pension Tracing Service and MoneyHelper's four steps

Gov.uk's Pension Tracing Service finds contact details for "your own workplace or personal pension scheme", or someone else's with their permission. Be clear about its limits: "This service will not tell you whether you have a pension, or what its value is." It gives you the scheme's current address so you can ask. You need "the name of an employer or a pension provider", and you can search online or ring 0800 731 0175 (Monday to Friday, 10am to 3pm).

MoneyHelper sets out a sensible four-step order:

1. **List previous employers.**
2. **Identify the pension providers**, from old paperwork, by asking the employer, or through a free tracing service.
3. **Use the Pension Tracing Service** to get up-to-date contact details.
4. **Contact the providers directly** with enough information to match you to their records.

Providers will typically ask for your National Insurance number, previous names and addresses, employment dates and roughly when the pension was set up. Two tips from the same guide: if the provider has changed its name or been taken over, the Association of British Insurers keeps a record of who became whom; and if your old employer went bust, its scheme may now be with the Pension Protection Fund. MoneyHelper is also offering early access to a pensions dashboard, described as "a clear view of your pensions"; worth signing up for, but list-and-trace is the reliable route for now.

## The US: Lost and Found, PBGC and unclaimed property

The US has a central government database. The Department of Labor's Retirement Savings Lost and Found, created by the SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022, is "a centralized location to find lost or forgotten benefits". You verify your identity through Login.gov (legal name, date of birth, Social Security number, a phone and photos of your state driving licence), and it shows retirement plans linked to you from private employers and unions, both traditional pensions and 401(k)-type plans, with the administrator's contact details.

One caveat, from the site itself: "Your benefits may have already been paid out, rolled over into another retirement account, or provided as an annuity." A match means a plan once had you on its books; only the administrator can confirm money is waiting.

If a former employer's pension plan ended, try the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation's unclaimed retirement benefits search, which covers private-sector plans that terminated and lost touch with people. You search by last name and the last four digits of your Social Security number.

Finally, uncashed cheques and small balances can end up with state unclaimed property offices. The National Association of Unclaimed Property Administrators, a network of state treasurers, points to MissingMoney.com as the free way to search several states at once, and to a States Unclaimed Retirement Clearinghouse for "uncashed retirement distribution checks". The searches are free; anyone charging is selling you something you can do yourself.

## Should you combine what you find?

It is tempting, once you have four small pots, to sweep them into one. MoneyHelper lists the upsides: a possible "better deal, including cheaper fees and more options to invest or take your money", and simpler admin. The downsides are the ones people miss.

Before moving any UK pension, check:

- **Exit fees.** Does the old scheme charge you to leave?
- **Guarantees.** Some older pensions have "guaranteed annuity rates", which "might let you convert your pension into a higher guaranteed income than you could get elsewhere". Others carry with-profits bonuses or a protected earlier retirement age. Transfer and these are gone.
- **Employer contributions.** If it is a current scheme, your employer may not pay into a replacement.
- **The £30,000 rule.** If a pension guarantees you an income and those "guaranteed benefits are worth more than £30,000", you must take regulated financial advice before transferring. That rule exists because these are usually the pots worth keeping.
- **Small pots.** Pots under £10,000 may fall under "small pot rules" allowing a lump sum with 25% tax-free; a different decision from consolidating.

The same logic applies in the US to rolling an old 401(k) into an IRA: compare fees, check what you would give up, and be wary of anyone enthusiastic before they have seen the old plan's paperwork.

## This week

1. Write the employer list, badly and quickly.
2. UK: run the Pension Tracing Service for each name. US: search the Retirement Savings Lost and Found, then PBGC and MissingMoney.com.
3. Write to each provider with your NI or Social Security number, old addresses and dates, asking for a value and whether there are guarantees.
4. Put the answers in one table: provider, type, value, guarantees, reference number.
5. Do not transfer anything yet. Sit with the table for a month.
