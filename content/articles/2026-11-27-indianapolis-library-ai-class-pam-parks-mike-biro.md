At the Nora Branch of the Indianapolis Public Library, students in the free AI class worked on library Chromebooks. Their teacher, Janna Kreider, a tech learning specialist, had them try ChatGPT or Google Gemini on ordinary jobs: a grocery list, a meal plan. Then she showed them how to test what came back.

"That's the simplest thing you can do," she told the class on Feb. 10, according to Mirror Indy, a local Indianapolis newsroom. "You can at least see, does this thing that the AI is referencing actually exist?"

Pam Parks, 55, took one of the library's classes. She had used ChatGPT only a couple of times before, and her sister had encouraged her to try it. She hoped that AI skills might help her toward a raise or a promotion.

> "I want to try to learn as much as I can, to see if it'll help me benefit."
> — Pam Parks, to Mirror Indy

Mike Biro, 56, of McCordsville, already used ChatGPT to tailor his résumé for each job he applied for. The class widened his idea of what it could do.

> "It's really opening me up to different things."
> — Mike Biro, to Mirror Indy

## Two classes, both free

The library added the classes because patrons asked for them, Mirror Indy reported. There are two: a general introduction to generative AI (the kind that writes text or makes pictures in answer to a request), and a class on recognizing content that AI has made. The introduction explains terms such as "large language model" and "generative AI", then moves on to practice.

Kreider's aim is for students to "walk away knowing how to get what they need" even if the class did not answer their question. Another of the library's tech learning specialists, Dan Snodgrass, said he wants people to feel more confident talking about AI, because the less mysterious it is, the less it seems like magic.

His tip for video is easy to remember. AI video generators are good at short clips, he said, so treat anything 10 seconds or shorter with more skepticism. The class also taught students to follow the links in AI summaries and to look for watermarks or blurring.

## Why this matters

Not everyone at the Nora Branch was there to get ahead at work. Donna Meek, 78, came with real fears about AI. She told Mirror Indy she did not expect to use the tools much more, but she left feeling less afraid and more empowered.

Telling real from made-up is not easy for anyone. Mirror Indy cited a 2025 Pew Research Center survey in which more than half of Americans were not confident they could tell AI-generated content from content made by people.

In the UK, Age UK (the national charity for older people) gives the same two warnings in its guide to AI. AI can make convincing fake articles, photos and videos, so check a trusted news source before you believe or share one. And never give passwords, bank account numbers or contact details to a public AI tool.

## How to start

Libraries are the easy way in. Indianapolis is not alone: Nashville Public Library has run free AI classes in many of its branches, NewsChannel 5 reported, covering everyday uses such as recipes and trip planning, with safety tips. Ask at your own library's front desk or on its events page. In the UK, ask your library, or your local Age UK about its digital support.
