Required minimum distributions, or RMDs, are the yearly amounts you must take out of a traditional IRA or 401(k) once you reach a set age. Get the timing right and they are just another withdrawal; miss one and the penalty is steep.

This is general information, not tax or financial advice. The questions in this guide are ones to take to a CPA or a fee-only adviser.

## When withdrawals have to start

The IRS says RMDs [generally begin at age 73](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plan-and-ira-required-minimum-distributions-faqs). It moves later for younger savers. In the IRS's wording from a 2026 notice, for someone who reaches age 73 before January 1, 2033, "the applicable age is 73," and for someone "who attains age 74 after December 31, 2032, the applicable age is 75" ([Notice 2026-13](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-26-13.pdf)). If you are near that line, ask a CPA which age applies to you.

**The first deadline is different.** You "can delay taking the first RMD until April 1 of the following year," says the IRS, and the second is then due by December 31 of that same year. Its example: turn 73 in 2024, and the first RMD is due by April 1, 2025, and the second by December 31, 2025. Every later one is due by December 31. Delaying the first means two taxable withdrawals in one year: worth asking a CPA about.

**Working past your RMD age.** In a workplace plan such as a 401(k), you can delay RMDs until you retire, "unless they're a 5% owner of the business sponsoring the plan." The IRS describes this delay for workplace retirement plans only; owners of traditional, SEP and SIMPLE IRAs "must begin taking RMDs once the account holder is age 73."

**Roth accounts.** Roth IRAs, and designated Roth accounts in 401(k) and 403(b) plans, have no RMDs during the owner's lifetime, the IRS says. People who inherit them do face RMD rules.

## How the amount is worked out

Your RMD is generally the account balance on December 31 of the year before, divided by a life-expectancy figure from the tables in [Publication 590-B](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590b). Most owners use the Uniform Lifetime Table (Table III); the Joint and Last Survivor Table is for owners whose spouse is the sole beneficiary and more than 10 years younger.

**A hypothetical example.** Suppose you turn 73 this year and your traditional IRA was worth $300,000 last December 31. The Uniform Lifetime Table gives an applicable denominator of 26.5 at age 73. By our arithmetic, $300,000 ÷ 26.5 = about $11,320.75, the least you would need to take out for that year. (At 74 the table's figure is 25.5, and at 75 it is 24.6, so the share rises each year.)

**More than one account.** You work out the RMD for each IRA separately but can take the total from one or more of your IRAs; 403(b) contracts work the same way. RMDs from 401(k) and 457(b) plans "must be taken separately from each of those plan accounts," the IRS says.

## The penalty for a missed RMD, and how to fix it

If you take less than your RMD, the shortfall "may be subject to an excise tax of 25%," or "10% if the RMD is timely corrected within two years," says the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plan-and-ira-required-minimum-distributions-faqs). It is reported on Form 5329 with your return for the year the RMD was missed.

The tax can be waived if the shortfall was due to "reasonable error" and you are taking reasonable steps to put it right. To ask, you file Form 5329 and attach a letter of explanation. How to word the letter is a question for a CPA.

## Taking money out early

Withdraw before 59½ and you may owe a 10% additional tax on top of ordinary income tax, unless an exception applies ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plans-faqs-regarding-iras-distributions-withdrawals)). One exception matters to many early retirees: for workplace plans such as a 401(k), it does not apply to payments made after "the employee separates from service during or after the year the employee reaches age 55" ([IRS exceptions chart](https://irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-exceptions-to-tax-on-early-distributions)). That exception does **not** apply to IRAs, and for certain state and local public safety employees the age is 50. Other exceptions include death, total and permanent disability, and a series of substantially equal payments.

| | Traditional IRA | Roth IRA | 401(k) or 403(b) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **When RMDs start** | Generally 73 (75 for those who reach 74 after 2032, per IRS wording) | No RMDs in the owner's lifetime | Same starting age; Roth money in the plan has no lifetime RMDs |
| **Still-working exception** | No | Not needed | Yes, until you retire, unless you are a 5% owner |
| **10% early tax before 59½** | Can apply; the age-55 separation rule does not cover IRAs | Can apply; IRS says "certain distributions from Roth IRAs are not taxable" | Can apply, but not to payments after leaving a job in or after the year you turn 55 |
| **Combining RMDs** | Total can come from one or more IRAs | n/a | 403(b)s can be combined; 401(k)s must be taken plan by plan |
| **Qualified charitable distributions** | Yes, from 70½ | IRS mentions QCDs from a Roth IRA (extra Form 8606 rules) | No; the IRS describes QCDs as distributions from an IRA |

## Giving to charity straight from your IRA

A qualified charitable distribution, or QCD, is an otherwise taxable distribution from an IRA (other than an ongoing SEP or SIMPLE IRA) owned by someone age 70½ or over, "paid directly from the IRA to a qualified charity," says the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plans-faqs-regarding-iras-distributions-withdrawals). A QCD "can satisfy all or part" of your RMD. On your Form 1040 you report the full amount on the IRA distributions line, enter zero as the taxable amount if all of it was a QCD, and write "QCD" next to it.

The yearly limit rises with inflation. The IRS's [Notice 2026-66](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-26-66.pdf) says it increases from $111,000 for 2026 to $114,000 for 2027. A separate one-time election to give through a split-interest entity rises from $55,000 to $57,000. Whether a QCD beats an ordinary gift for you is a good question for a CPA.

## Tax withholding

The IRS's [Form W-4R](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/fw4r.pdf) sets the defaults. For one-off (nonperiodic) payments, such as most IRA withdrawals, "the default withholding rate is 10%," and you can choose any rate from 0% to 100%. For an eligible rollover distribution from a plan, the default is 20% and you can't choose less; RMDs are not eligible rollover distributions for these rules. Payments sent outside the US generally can't have less than 10% withheld.

Ask a CPA whether your withholding will cover the bill, especially in a year with two RMDs. ## If you inherit an IRA

For owners who died after December 31, 2019, the whole balance generally must be paid out within ten years, the [IRS says](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plan-and-ira-required-minimum-distributions-faqs). Exceptions include a surviving spouse, a minor child, a disabled or chronically ill person, and anyone not more than ten years younger than the owner.

## Questions people ask

**At what age do I have to take money out of my IRA?** Generally 73, the IRS says, with the first withdrawal allowed to wait until April 1 of the following year. The IRS describes a later age, 75, for people who reach 74 after December 31, 2032.

**What happens if I miss my RMD?** The shortfall may face a 25% excise tax, cut to 10% if corrected within two years. You can ask for a waiver on Form 5329 with a letter of explanation.

**Do I have to take RMDs from a Roth IRA or Roth 401(k)?** Not during your lifetime, according to the IRS. Beneficiaries who inherit them do.

**Can I give my RMD to charity?** From an IRA, from age 70½, a qualified charitable distribution paid directly to a qualified charity can count toward it, up to $111,000 for 2026.
