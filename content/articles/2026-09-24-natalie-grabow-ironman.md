An Ironman is a 3.8 km open-water swim, a 180 km bike ride and then a full 26.2-mile marathon, one after the other, with a cut-off of 17 hours. It is the hardest one-day event most amateur athletes will ever attempt.

On 11 October 2025, in Kailua-Kona, Hawaii, Natalie Grabow of Mountain Lakes, New Jersey, finished the Ironman World Championship in 16 hours 45 minutes 26 seconds. She was 80 years old — the oldest woman ever to complete the race.

## The bit that matters: she couldn't swim at 59

Grabow's story is usually told from the finish line. Told from the start, it is more useful.

At 59, she could not swim. She wanted to try triathlon, so she learned. She raced her first sprint-distance triathlon in 2005. Over the following twenty-one years, according to Guinness World Records, she completed roughly 30 sprint and Olympic-distance races, around 50 half-Ironmans and 16 full Ironmans.

> "Over the past 21 years as a triathlete, I've completed about 30 sprint and Olympic distance races…"
> — Natalie Grabow, Guinness World Records

The maths there is worth pausing on. She spent a full year or two just learning to swim and doing short events. Then she spent a decade in the middle distances. The world record came in year twenty-one. Nobody does this fast.

## How she trains: study the competition

Her coach, Michelle Lake, has said that Grabow studies not only her age-group competitors but also the male athletes in the same bracket. That is a very specific mindset: not "I'm doing well for my age", but "who is the best in the world at my age, and what are they doing?"

You do not need to want a world record to borrow that. Find the people doing your thing at your age *well*. Copy them.

## Why swimming is often the gateway

Notice that two of the most remarkable late-starting athletes we have featured this week — Grabow and [Betty Brussel](/stories/betty-brussel-99-three-world-records/) — began in the water. There is a reason. Swimming:

- carries your weight, so joints that would not survive running are fine
- can be learned properly as an adult with a few lessons
- has clubs almost everywhere that welcome beginners and "improvers"
- gives instant, measurable progress (lengths, then times)

## The realistic version

Most of you are not going to do an Ironman. That is not the point. The point is the sequence: she decided at 59, learned a basic skill, did small events, kept going, and the extraordinary thing arrived two decades later almost as a by-product. If you are 59 and thinking "it's a bit late to learn to swim", Grabow's story says the opposite: you may have twenty years of it ahead of you.
