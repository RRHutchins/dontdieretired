On 27 November 2025, a loaded touring bike rolled back into Bangkok. It had left the Thai capital in January and covered more than 30,000 km (18,641 miles) through 25 countries in between. Nobody was waiting at the finish.

The arrival felt more like ticking off a task than a triumph, Mark Herbst told Guinness World Records. The feeling came later. "Holy cow! I just rode around the world!"

Herbst, 69, a cycling coach from Orillia, Ontario, had just become the oldest man to circumnavigate the world by bicycle. The ride took 324 days.

## Why he went

Herbst and his wife, Jackie, met scuba diving and had 25 years together. She was an Ironman and a triathlon world championship competitor, he wrote on his coaching website, as Guinness reported. She died of cancer in December 2021, aged 55.

"I was trying to find a sense of purpose in my life," he told Global News. He had raced ultra-endurance events before, and after Jackie's death he won several provincial and national titles. Then racing stopped being enough.

> "I'd ridden by the entrance to the Grand Canyon four times and I still hadn't seen it, because I was racing."
> — Mark Herbst, to Guinness World Records

If he was going to ride around the world, he said, he wanted to see some of it.

## The road

He rode alone, on what his friend Barrie Shepley, an Olympic triathlon coach, called "a 100-plus pound bike", carrying everything. Global News reported he was riding between eight and 12 hours a day. The route took him through Thailand, Malaysia and Australia, through Spain, France and central Europe, and across the United States, including Arizona and Route 66. Turkey, Albania, Slovenia and Georgia were all new to him.

What stayed with him was the people. In Australia, he said, strangers he had never met emailed to offer their homes while they were away. In Western Australia a woman offered him a place to sleep at her café.

> "I really didn't know what to expect, people were just so friendly."
> — Mark Herbst, to Global News

He scattered some of Jackie's ashes in places they had wanted to visit together, and raised money for the Princess Margaret Cancer Foundation. Guinness reports a cheque for $67,746 CAD (about £36,000 or US$48,000). The record had first gone to Christopher Bennett, a New Zealander who finished at 66; the two men messaged and cheered each other on.

## Why this matters

Herbst is clear that the size of the trip is not the point. "It doesn't have to be a ride around the world," he told Global News. Learning a language or the guitar would do, he said: the point is to keep challenging yourself, so you keep growing.

He is not stopping, either. Guinness lists skiing, hiking, diving, guitar, a motorcycle licence, CrossFit and a book about the ride among his plans.

## How to start a cycle tour

Touring is endurance at a pace you can hold all day, which is why it suits people who are not racers. The step that matters most is not fitness but the first night away: once you have ridden somewhere, slept and ridden on, a longer trip is the same thing repeated.

Build in stages. Ride a route you know with the bags you would carry. Then do two days back to back, then a long weekend. Keep your daily distance below what you can comfortably ride unloaded, and plan rest days from the start. Signed routes take care of the navigation; our guide to [riding a long-distance cycle route](/list/long-distance-cycle-route/) links to the National Cycle Network route finder in the UK and the Adventure Cycling Association's maps in the US.

If you would rather walk your long distance, Nancy Zelick [took seven months over the Pacific Crest Trail](/explore/nancy-zelick-pacific-crest-trail/), and Steve James [ran a marathon a day around Britain](/stories/steve-james-200-marathons-coastline/).
