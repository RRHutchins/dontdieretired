"Drink when you're thirsty" is perfectly good advice at 30. After 50 it quietly stops working, because the thirsty part becomes unreliable. At the same time, the glass of wine that used to be nothing starts to matter more. Neither is a crisis. Both are worth knowing about.

This is general information, not medical advice. If you have a heart, kidney or bladder condition, or take medication that affects fluid balance, ask your GP (family doctor) or a registered dietitian how much you should drink.

## Why thirst gets quieter

The British Dietetic Association (BDA) says older people "may not recognise the feeling of thirst in the same way" as younger people. The US National Library of Medicine is blunter: "Some people lose their sense of thirst as they age." The US Dietary Guidelines' notes for older adults call dehydration "a significant risk" for the same reason, along with worries about bladder control that lead some people to drink less on purpose.

So the signal you have relied on all your life fades, and by the time you feel dry you may already be behind. The BDA also warns that the "common signs of dehydration do not work in older adults"; instead it can show up as headaches, tiredness, confusion, poor concentration, constipation or urinary tract infections — things easily blamed on other causes.

## How much, and how to make it automatic

The NHS Eatwell Guide recommends six to eight cups or glasses of fluid a day. The BDA gives volumes: at least 1,600ml (3 pints, about 54 US fl oz) a day for women and at least 2,000ml (3½ pints, about 68 US fl oz) for men, more in hot weather. Age UK adds that "this doesn't have to be water — milk, soups, tea and coffee all count."

The trick is to swap thirst for routine. The BDA's own suggestions: a drink with your medication, a drink with every meal, a drink as part of any social activity, and a cup or glass you like and can hold comfortably. A few more:

- **Anchor drinks to things you already do.** Kettle on when you get up. A mug when you sit down for the news.
- **Keep it visible.** A full glass on the worktop gets drunk. A bottle in the fridge does not.
- **Eat your fluids.** Soup, stews, fruit, yoghurt and vegetables all count.
- **Check the colour.** The NHS suggests pale yellow urine as the sign you are drinking enough.
- **Front-load the day** if getting up at night is a worry, rather than cutting back overall — and mention it to your GP.

## Alcohol: the UK guideline

The UK Chief Medical Officers' low-risk guideline, as set out by the NHS, is the same for men and women: no more than 14 units a week on a regular basis, spread over three or more days, with several drink-free days each week if you are cutting down.

The NHS figures for common drinks are:

- A single 25ml measure of spirits (40%): 1 unit
- A small 125ml glass of wine (12%): 1.5 units
- A pint of lower-strength beer (3.6%): 2 units
- A pint of higher-strength beer (5.2%): 3 units
- A large 250ml glass of wine (12%): 3 units

Two large glasses of wine a night, most nights, is past 14 units by Thursday. A pint of strong lager is not "a drink" in the guideline's terms — it is three.

## Alcohol: the US guidelines

The 2020–2025 US Dietary Guidelines, as summarised by the National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism, say adults who drink should keep to "two drinks or less in a day for men and one drink or less in a day for women". The notes for older adults add that alcohol carries a "greater fall and accident risk" with age. The 2025–2030 edition drops the numbers and says simply: "Consume less alcohol for better overall health."

Whichever side of the Atlantic you are on, the direction is the same: less is better, and "a drink" is smaller than most people pour.

## Why drink-free days work

Spreading drinking over three or more days, with several drink-free days, does two jobs. It stops the units piling into one or two heavy sessions, and it breaks the habit of a drink being automatic at six o'clock.

Drink-free days are the easiest lever to pull. You do not give anything up permanently or count anything; you decide in advance that Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday are dry. Have something you actually enjoy in the fridge for those evenings — a good cordial, alcohol-free beer, sparkling water with lime — so it is not a matter of going without. The two subjects meet here: alcohol is not a good way to get your fluids, and a run of dry days tends to mean more water, more tea and better sleep.

## When to talk to a GP

Have a word with your GP or pharmacist if:

- You are unsure how much to drink because of a heart, kidney or bladder condition, or your medication.
- You get dizzy when you stand up, or have had a fall.
- You have unexplained tiredness, confusion or repeated urinary infections — the BDA lists these as possible signs of dehydration in older adults.
- You are drinking more alcohol than you meant to, or the drink-free days are harder than expected. That is a common conversation, not an embarrassing one.
