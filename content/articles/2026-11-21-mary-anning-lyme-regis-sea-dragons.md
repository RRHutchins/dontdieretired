Some time around 1811, under Church Cliffs at Lyme Regis in Dorset, Joseph Anning found a huge fossil skull. His younger sister Mary went back to the spot and spent months digging out the rest of the animal: a skeleton about five metres (16 feet) long. Scientists first took it for a crocodile.

It was an ichthyosaur, a sea reptile that lived about 200 million years ago. Mary Anning was about 12. Over the next 35 years she would find creatures stranger still, and help persuade the scientific world of something it was reluctant to accept: that whole kinds of animal had once lived and then vanished.

## A family trade on the beach

Mary Anning was born in Lyme Regis in 1799. Her father, Richard, was a cabinetmaker and an amateur collector who taught her to find and clean fossils, the Natural History Museum says, and sold them to visitors. The Lyme Regis Museum says the family sold their finds from a table outside their home.

Richard died in 1810. The Smithsonian says the family then lived in poverty and on parish relief, and the Natural History Museum says Mary's mother encouraged her to sell her finds to pay the family's debts. The ichthyosaur sold for £23, according to the Lyme Regis Museum, to Henry Hoste Henley, lord of the manor of Colway.

The sources do not quite agree on dates. The Natural History Museum says Joseph found the skull in the autumn of 1811 and Mary uncovered the skeleton from November 1812. The Lyme Regis Museum puts the find in the year after her father's death. The Smithsonian gives "sometime between 1809 and 1811", when she was 10 to 12.

## The sea dragon nobody believed

In December 1823 Anning found the first complete skeleton of a plesiosaur, a sea reptile with a small head on a very long neck. The French anatomist Georges Cuvier, the leading expert of the day, suspected it was a fake or a composite of several animals. The Geological Society in London held a special meeting to discuss it. Anning was not invited.

Cuvier was eventually convinced, and later bought a plesiosaur from her for the museum in Paris. The Natural History Museum says the find helped show that the Earth is very old and that animals can go extinct.

In December 1828 she found jumbled bones with wings and a long tail: the first pterosaur, or flying reptile, found outside Germany, now called Dimorphodon. William Buckland, the Oxford geologist who described it, wrote that it resembled "nothing that has ever been seen or heard-of upon earth, excepting the dragons of romance and heraldry."

She was also among the first to study coprolites, fossil droppings, and found fish scales and bones inside them.

## Bought, named and left out

Men of science bought her fossils and often left her name out of their papers, even for the first ichthyosaur, the Natural History Museum says. The Geological Society would not admit her. Here, too, the sources differ: the Natural History Museum says women could not become Fellows until 1908, the Lyme Regis Museum says 1919.

Her friend the geologist Henry De la Beche painted a scene of ancient Dorset sea life in 1830 and sold prints to raise money for her. Her only known published writing is a letter of 1839 to the Magazine of Natural History, correcting its editor. She died in Lyme Regis in 1847, aged 47. The Lyme Regis Museum says the Geological Society later honoured her with a stained-glass window in St Michael's Church, near her grave. Charles Dickens, quoted by the Smithsonian, wrote: "The carpenter's daughter has won a name for herself, and has deserved to win it."

## Two stories to take with a pinch of salt

Lyme tells of a lightning strike when Mary was a baby, sheltering under a tree with her nurse. The Lyme Regis Museum says she was 15 months old and that the strike killed her nurse and two others; the Smithsonian says she was 14 months old and the only survivor of five. Local lore held that a dull baby grew up bright afterwards. Her survival is the fact; the change of character is folklore.

Then there is the tongue twister "She sells sea shells by the sea shore". The Smithsonian says it is based on her. Neither the Natural History Museum nor the Lyme Regis Museum pages we read make that claim, and none of the three gives evidence for it. Enjoy it as a legend.

## Where she is now

The Lyme Regis Museum, built in 1902, stands on the site of her birthplace and first fossil shop. In 2022 a statue of Anning and her dog was unveiled in the town after a campaign started by Evie Swire, then 11. In London, the Natural History Museum shows her ichthyosaur skull and plesiosaur in its Fossil Marine Reptiles gallery, and a cast of the skull that visitors can touch.

The beaches she worked still give up fossils every winter, when storms wash them out of the cliffs.
