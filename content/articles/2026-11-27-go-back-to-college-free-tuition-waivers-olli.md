Across the US, public colleges let older residents sit in on real university classes, often without paying tuition. The rules are set state by state, and sometimes college by college, so the age, the cost and the small print all vary.

This is general information, not financial advice. Everything below is as listed on 27 Nov 2026 on the official page linked; rules change, so check with the college before you plan a term around it.

## What a tuition waiver is

A tuition waiver means the college does not charge you tuition, the main cost of a class. In the programs below it usually comes with three conditions:

- **An age and residency test.** Typically 60, 62 or 65, and living in the state, often for at least a year.
- **Space available.** You get a seat only if one is left after paying students have registered.
- **Audit, or credit.** Auditing means you attend the class but earn no credit and no grade. Some states waive tuition only for auditing; others also cover credit courses, sometimes with an income limit.

Fees are a separate matter. Many waivers cover tuition only, so you may pay application, lab, parking or materials fees.

## A closer look at three

**Texas.** Texas State University's page cites a Texas law, Senate Bill 1210, which added a grade requirement. People 65 and over can take up to six credit hours a semester without tuition, keeping a 2.0 grade point average as an undergraduate. Auditing is free of tuition and fees, but you are first accepted by the university and need the department's approval.

**Ohio.** Ohio State's Program 60 is audit-only: no final grade and no credit hours. There is no published course list; you ask, and the instructor agrees if there is room. The FAQ says applications open three times a year.

**Massachusetts.** This one works like financial aid. The state's page says you must file the FAFSA (the federal student aid form) or the state MASFA, not be in default on a student loan, and make satisfactory academic progress.

## Osher Lifelong Learning Institutes: classes without the registrar

If you want to learn with others but have no interest in exams, an Osher Lifelong Learning Institute (OLLI) may suit you better. The [National Resource Center for OLLIs](https://sps.northwestern.edu/oshernrc/about/osher-lifelong-learning-institute-network.html) at Northwestern University says there are 124 institutes across all 50 states and the District of Columbia, each part of a college or university and partly funded by the Bernard Osher Foundation.

- **Who:** programs are designed for people "50 and better."
- **How it works:** non-credit courses, no tests and no grades, learning "solely for the joy of learning."
- **Cost:** you pay a membership fee. Some institutes bundle everything into it; others charge separately for courses.
- **How to find one:** the resource center's site has a map of institute locations.

Each institute runs independently, so ask yours about fees and what it offers. Our page on how to [join a u3a or an OLLI](/list/join-u3a-or-olli/) has first steps.

## Free online, from home

edX's help center says most of its courses can be taken free on the [audit track](https://support.edx.org/hc/en-us/articles/1500003964681-What-is-the-audit-track): you get the video lectures, readings, ungraded practice and the discussion forums. You do not get graded assignments or a certificate, and access ends after the course's estimated length.

## The catches

- **You register last.** Florida Atlantic processes 60+ requests after the regular Drop/Add period, by seat availability and the order forms arrive. Popular classes may be full.
- **Fees apply.** Parking, labs, materials and applications are the usual extras.
- **Proof of age and residency.** Georgia asks for a driver's license or similar document; Florida Atlantic needs a residency affidavit with supporting documents.
- **Admission first.** At Georgia, George Mason and Texas State, you apply and are admitted before the waiver applies.
- **Financial aid rules differ.** Texas State says audited courses are not eligible for financial aid.

## Five questions to ask the registrar

1. Do you have a tuition waiver for older residents, and what is the minimum age?
2. Is it for audit only, or credit too?
3. Which fees will I pay?
4. When can I register, and how do I know if a seat is free?
5. What proof of age and residency do you need, and do I have to reapply each term?

## FAQ

**Can older adults go to college for free?**
In a number of states, yes, for tuition at public colleges, usually on a space-available basis. Fees are often charged, and some programs are audit-only. Check the registrar's page at your nearest public college.

**What age can you go to college for free in Texas?**
At Texas State University, 65: you can audit free of tuition and fees, or take up to six credit hours a semester without tuition. Ask your own Texas college how it applies the rules.

**What is OLLI?**
An Osher Lifelong Learning Institute: a non-credit, membership-based program for people 50 and over, based at a college or university, with no tests or grades.

**Do I get credit?**
Only if your program allows credit courses. Auditing, and OLLI courses, earn no credit. Texas, Georgia, Virginia, Illinois, Massachusetts and Washington list credit options, with conditions.
