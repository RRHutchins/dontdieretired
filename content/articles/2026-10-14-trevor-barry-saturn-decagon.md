In mid-2024 Trevor Barry was taking pictures of Saturn from a dome in his back yard in Broken Hill, in outback New South Wales, when he noticed a pattern of bright spots near the planet's south pole. He described it to the professor he works with as a ripple.

It was a wave. In September the journal *Science Advances* published a paper describing a ten-sided shape, a decagon, circling Saturn's south pole. ABC News reports that it is the first large jet pattern seen in the planet's southern hemisphere. Barry, a former mine worker in his 70s who left school after Year 10, is one of the authors.

> "All the other authors are professors or PhDs but there's Trevor the mine worker from Broken Hill."
> — Trevor Barry, to ABC News

## A dome built from a water tank

Barry spent 30 years in Broken Hill's mines as an electrical machinist and fitter, according to National Seniors Australia. He took up stargazing seriously in the early 2000s, then studied for a Graduate Certificate of Science in astronomy online with Swinburne University of Technology. He graduated in 2005 with straight High Distinctions and the university's award for the top graduating student on the course.

He designed and built his own observatory and several telescopes. The dome took about a decade and is made partly from a rainwater tank and a second-hand washing machine motor.

Since 2008 he has worked with Agustín Sánchez-Lavega, a planetary scientist at the University of the Basque Country in Spain. That first year, Swinburne records, Barry photographed a white spot on Saturn that turned out to be an electrical storm NASA researchers were already studying.

## Never mind ripple

Barry sent the new images to Spain.

> "Agustín got back to me, and he said, 'Well, Trev, never mind ripple, think wave'."
> — Trevor Barry, to ABC News

Barry turned his pictures into polar projections, maps that look straight down on the pole. He told ABC News the detail showed only on nights of very steady air. A French amateur, Jean-Paul Oger, also picked up the band, Smithsonian Magazine reports, and Hubble images taken in 2023 by Amy Simon of NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center had caught its beginnings.

The shape recalls the hexagon at Saturn's north pole, found by the Voyager flybys in 1980 and 1981. Saturn's south pole was turned away from Earth from 2012 to 2023, so nobody knows exactly when the decagon formed, why, or whether it will last. Barry plans to keep watching.

> "He'll track it over time, and that really makes it a lot easier for us to say 'yes, that is real'."
> — Amy Simon, NASA, to ABC News

## Why this matters

Large telescopes look at Saturn now and then. Someone with a dome in the garden can look on every clear night for years, and Simon told ABC News that what Barry's observatory gives is "very consistent results, high quality images".

Looking up may also do something for the looker. In a study led from the University of California, San Francisco, 52 volunteers took a 15-minute walk at least once a week for eight weeks. Half were asked to look out for things that gave them a sense of awe. That group reported more compassion and gratitude in daily surveys; the other half, who walked more often, showed no such change. It was a small study, and the authors called the effects moderate.

## Your own look at Saturn

Saturn was at opposition on 4 October, BBC Sky at Night Magazine reports: opposite the Sun, so it is up all night. With the naked eye it is an off-white point that does not twinkle. The rings need a telescope, and the magazine suggests a 75mm (3-inch) refractor or larger. They are opening as Saturn's south pole tips towards Earth, the same tilt that brought the decagon into view.
