Ravi Sood, from Canberra, was in his seventies and facing a double knee replacement when his surgeon gave him a piece of advice that most patients would file under "get through it". Sood filed it under "get ready".

> "Both my knees needed replacing, and the orthopaedic surgeon said that if I had my muscles in good condition, the surgery would be easier."
> — Ravi Sood, ABC News

So, at 70, he started weightlifting. Twelve years later, at 82, he holds the Australian deadlift record for his age and weight class — 115 kilograms — and was selected to represent Australia at the World Masters Powerlifting Championships in South Africa.

> "This is my dream come true. Just the fact that I'm wearing Australian green and gold, for me, that's fantastic."

## "Prehab" is real, and under-used

Surgeons and physiotherapists have known for years that patients who go into joint surgery stronger come out of it faster. The idea has a name — prehabilitation — and it is one of the best-evidenced reasons for an older adult to start strength training. Stronger quadriceps and glutes mean a quicker return to walking, fewer complications and less time in rehab afterwards.

What Sood's story shows is what happens if you *don't stop* once the surgery is done. The habit that was meant to be a means to an end became the end itself.

## A note on motivation

Sood is not chasing medals.

> "I don't really care if I win any medals — just to represent my country at an international level, that's out of this world."

Notice the pattern across this week's stories. [Martine Barons](/stories/martine-barons-world-champion-deadlift-65/) talks about lifting grandchildren, not trophies. [Betty Brussel](/stories/betty-brussel-99-three-world-records/) talks about being with people. Sood talks about wearing the green and gold. None of them is motivated by the number on the bar. The number is a by-product of turning up.

## Why deadlifting suits older bodies

The deadlift — picking a weight up off the floor with a straight back and standing up — is the most functional lift there is. It is literally the movement of lifting a grandchild, a shopping bag or a suitcase. Done with good coaching and sensible loads:

- it strengthens the back, hips and legs together
- it improves grip strength, one of the best predictors of healthy ageing
- it loads the bones, which matters enormously for osteoporosis risk
- it is straightforward to progress: add a little weight, or a rep, each week

Sood started at 70 with a surgeon's suggestion and a pair of knees that needed replacing. If that is not permission to start, we are not sure what would be.
