## Why this works

The free AI classes at the Indianapolis Public Library, Mirror Indy reported, start with ordinary jobs such as a grocery list or a meal plan, then spend real time on checking. The teacher, Janna Kreider, told students the simplest test is to ask: "does this thing that the AI is referencing actually exist?" Her colleague Dan Snodgrass warned that AI is good at making short video clips, so a clip of 10 seconds or less deserves extra doubt.

This plan copies that order. You use a free assistant on tasks where a mistake costs nothing, then on tasks that matter, and you check every time. Two habits run through every week:

1. **Check what it tells you.** An AI assistant can give a confident answer that is wrong. Open the source it names, or find one yourself, before you rely on anything.
2. **Keep private details out.** Never type passwords, bank or card numbers, your address, or medical details into an AI tool. Age UK gives the same advice in its guide to AI.

## Before you start

- **One free AI assistant.** ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot and Gemini all have free versions at the time of writing; any one will do. Our guide to [your first conversation with an AI assistant](/ai/your-first-conversation-with-an-ai-assistant/) covers signing up.
- **A device with a keyboard if you can.** The Indianapolis class used library laptops; many libraries lend them or have computers you can book.
- **A notebook page** headed: date, task, what it said, how I checked, right / partly right / wrong.
- **A free class, if there is one near you.** Ask your library (US or UK), or in the UK your local Age UK about its digital support. A teacher in the room makes week 1 much easier.

## The plan

| Week | Do this | How often | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (new to this) | Everyday tasks: ask for a grocery list, a week of meals, a packing list for a trip. Ask it to change one thing each time ("no fish", "for two people"). | 4 days, 15 minutes | Nothing here can go badly wrong. The point is to get used to asking and asking again. |
| 1 (already using it) | Pick one real piece of work you do often (a letter, a résumé, a report) and write a clear request for it, saying who it is for and how long it should be. | 3 sessions | Save your best request; you will reuse it in week 3. |
| 2 (new to this) | Ask three questions you already know the answers to. Then ask "Where does that come from?" and open what it names. | 4 days, 20 minutes | Note whether the source exists and whether it says what the AI said. |
| 2 (already using it) | The ten-question test: ten factual questions on a subject you know well, each checked against a source you trust. | Once, about an hour | Score right, partly right, wrong. This is your baseline. |
| 3 (new to this) | One real task, such as a polite email to a company or a summary of a long letter. Read every line before you use it. | 2 or 3 times | Remove anything you did not ask for or cannot confirm. |
| 3 (already using it) | Use it on a real job, as Mike Biro does with his résumé, then check every date, name and claim against your own records before it goes anywhere. | 3 times | Mark each fact you had to correct. |
| 4 (both) | Spotting fakes: look at five short videos or photos shared online. For each, check its length, look for watermarks or blurring, and search for the story on a news site you trust. | 3 or 4 sessions | Read our guide to [spotting AI fakes](/ai/how-to-spot-ai-fakes-photos-videos-reviews/) first. |

## If it goes wrong

- **The answer is confident and wrong.** Good: that is the lesson. Write it down and tell the assistant it was wrong; see how it responds.
- **It names a source that does not exist.** Stop relying on that answer. Find a source yourself, from an official body, a library or a news site you trust.
- **It asks for personal details.** You never need to give your name, address, date of birth or any account number to get help with a list, a letter or a question.
- **You feel lost.** Go back to week 1 and stay there a while, or book a class at your library. Many people find a teacher sitting beside them makes it click.

## Track it

At the end of each week, count: tasks done, answers checked, and answers that were wrong or partly wrong. By week 4 you should be able to finish this sentence from your own notes: "I use AI for ___, and I always check ___."
