## Why this works

Dance asks body and brain to do several things at once: keep time, remember a sequence, shift weight, stay upright. That combination is why long-running studies have linked regular dancing with better balance and a lower rate of cognitive decline than most other leisure activities. You are learning while you move.

It also solves boredom, which ends most new exercise habits. A tap routine gives you eight counts to get right, a teacher watching, and music. The effort is real but your attention is on the steps.

The other ingredient is the room. A weekly class has a fixed time, a teacher who notices an empty spot and a handful of people who will ask where you were. That is worth more than any amount of good intention.

## Before you start

- If you have a heart condition, joint replacement, dizziness or a recent fall, check with your GP first. Tell the teacher too; they will adapt.
- Kit: nothing at first. Hard-soled shoes you can turn in; tap shoes later if you stay.
- Cost: adult beginner classes in the UK are typically £6–£12 a session, often with a free or half-price taster. Tap shoes, when you want them, are £25–£50 second-hand.
- Where: dance schools, community centres and church halls. Search "adult beginners tap" or "over 50s dance" with your town; the [ISTD](https://www.istd.org) lists schools and U3A groups run daytime classes. (In the US, community colleges, YMCA branches and your parks and recreation department.)
- Not tap? The plan below works for ballroom, line dancing, salsa, adult ballet or contemporary. Pick the one that makes you smile at the thought.

## The plan

| Week | Do this | How often | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Find three classes near you. Ring or email one and ask about a taster. Book it. | One afternoon of searching | Never danced, or "two left feet"? Say so when you book. Beginners' classes exist because of you. |
| 1 | At home: stand in the kitchen and step in time to one song. Heel-toe, heel-toe. | 5 minutes, 3 days | The aim is to get used to moving to music, not to be good. |
| 2 | Go to the taster. Stand at the back if you like. Stay for the whole class. | Once | Judge it on one thing: did you smile? |
| 2 | Practise the one step you remember, badly, at home. | 5 minutes, 3 days | Badly is fine. Memory is being built. |
| 3 | Go back. Say hello to one person by name. | Once | Week two is where most people stop. Book week three before you leave week two. |
| 3 | Watch a short online video of the basic step (shuffle, ball change) and copy it. | 10 minutes, 3 days | Slower than the video is correct. |
| 4 | Third class. Ask the teacher what to work on. | Once | You now have a routine, a room and a reason. |
| 4 | Decide: this class, a different style, or a different night. Book a block. | Once | A block booking is a habit. |

## If it goes wrong

- **The class is too fast.** Ask whether there is an absolute beginners' or "silver" class. If not, stand at the back for a term; teachers expect it.
- **You feel foolish.** Everyone in that room felt foolish in their first month. Look at who is laughing: they are enjoying it. Join in.
- **Your knees or hips complain.** Soft-shoe rather than hard tap, or a low-impact style like ballroom. Mention it to the teacher and your GP if it persists.
- **You missed a week and now it feels awkward to go back.** It is not. Send the teacher a message saying you are coming. Then go.
