## Why this works

Kevin Marshall and Danny Wood won the men's 60 to 64 title as a pair at the 2026 Hyrox World Championships. Their coach, Matt Luxton, called them "local men with jobs, families and everyday commitments". They trained over months at a center in Lifton, on Saturday sessions shared with complete beginners, and their other coach, Charlie Wevill, found the weak spots and worked on them.

This plan copies those habits: a partner, a regular group session if you can find one, and honest work on whatever you find hardest. A Hyrox race is eight 1 km runs, each followed by a station: 1,000 m SkiErg, 50 m sled push, 50 m sled pull, 80 m burpee broad jumps, 1,000 m row, 200 m farmers carry, 100 m sandbag lunges and 100 wall balls. In doubles you run together and split each station as you choose. The training meets both halves of the guidelines from the CDC and the NHS: at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week, and strengthening for all the major muscle groups on at least two days.

## Before you start

- **Check with a GP (family doctor) or your doctor first** if you have not exercised for some time or have a medical condition.
- **Find the kit.** A gym with a rowing machine and, ideally, a SkiErg and a sled. Many gyms that run Hyrox classes call themselves Hyrox Training Clubs. Wear shoes with good grip.
- **Find a partner.** Someone of roughly your pace makes the runs easier to share. Agree early who is stronger at which station.
- **Learn the movements light.** Have a coach check your lunge, your wall-ball squat and how you lean into the sled before you add weight.
- **Look at the full format.** Our [Hyrox page](/list/do-a-hyrox/) lists the weights for each division and UK and US events.

## The plan

**How a week fits together.** Never put two hard days back to back. A hard day is a run at race pace, a station session or a practice race. Put a rest day or an easy walk between them, and keep at least two full rest days every week. The NHS's beginner running plan puts a rest day between every run, because rest is when the body recovers and adapts. Two easier weeks (weeks 4 and 7) and a lighter week before race day are built in. They are part of the plan, not time off it.

| Week | Do this | How often | Notes and why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 to 3 (getting going) | Walk or jog 20 to 30 minutes. Strength: sit-to-stands, a light farmers carry (3 x 20 m, about 65 feet), wall-ball squats with no ball. | 2 walks or jogs, 2 strength | Stop each set with a couple of reps left in the tank. Why: the first weeks build the habit and a base before any heavy or fast work. |
| 1 to 3 (already fit) | Runs: one steady 5 km, one session of 6 x 1 km at goal race pace. Strength: lunges, carries, rows, push-ups. | 3 runs, 2 strength | Note your 1 km times: they set your race pace. Why: running is half the race and most of the time on the course, so it starts first. |
| 4 (both) | Easier week: about half the usual time, same movements, lighter weights. Try the sled unloaded with a coach. | Half the usual sessions | Why: an easier week lets the tiredness of the first three clear, so you start the next block fresher. |
| 5 to 6 (getting going) | Add the rower: 3 x 250 m easy with walking rests. First "mini Hyrox" with your partner: 4 rounds of 400 m jog or brisk walk plus one light station each, shared. | Mini race once a week, plus 1 walk or jog and 2 strength | Push the sled with straight arms and short steps. Why: the race is about switching from running to lifting, and practicing it light teaches the switch before you add weight. |
| 5 to 6 (already fit) | Station practice after runs: 1 km, then one station at race weight. Rotate through all eight across the fortnight. | 3 runs, 2 strength | Find your slowest station. It gets extra work from now on. Why: it shows you where your training time is best spent. |
| 7 (both) | Easier week again: shorter sessions, lighter weights, no practice race. | Half the usual sessions | Let your legs recover. Why: the hardest block comes next, and you take it better rested. |
| 8 to 10 (getting going) | Build the mini race to 6 rounds of 500 m plus a station. Add a group class if you can. | Mini race once a week, plus 2 strength | Training beside others keeps it fun. Why: longer practice races build the stamina to keep going to the last station. |
| 8 to 10 (already fit) | Half-race simulation with your partner in week 8: 4 x (1 km + station), split as in a race. Full race simulation at race weights in week 10. Enter your race now. | One simulation every other week, plus 2 runs and 2 strength | Run the 1 km legs at the pace of the slower partner. Practice the hand-overs: they cost more time than you think. Why: a full simulation shows how your body copes late in the race, and leaving two weeks between them gives it time to recover. |
| 11 (both) | Taper: halve the volume, keep a little speed and one light station session. | 3 short sessions | Check your race-day kit and timings. Why: you arrive at the start fresh rather than tired from the last hard block. |
| 12 (getting going) | Finish an 8-round practice race (500 m plus a station each) with your partner. | Once | Time it. That is your first benchmark. |
| 12 (already fit) | Race a Hyrox doubles event. | Once | Start the first kilometer slower than you want to. Then take a few easy days before training again. |

## Look after your body

Hyrox suits people with full lives because it trains strength and stamina together. But it is hard, and the training is where people pick up injuries. These habits matter as much as the sessions.

- **Build slowly.** Add a little each week rather than a lot. In a 2014 Danish study of 874 new runners, those who added more than 30 percent a week had more injuries from running too far than those who added under 10 percent. The authors called the finding exploratory, but it is a sensible warning. If a week felt too hard, repeat it. The NHS's Couch to 5K plan says repeating a week is "totally okay".
- **Warm up every time.** The [NHS warm-up routine](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/how-to-warm-up-before-exercising/) takes at least six minutes: marching on the spot, heel digs, knee lifts, shoulder rolls and small knee bends. Do it before every run and station session.
- **Rest on purpose.** Keep at least two rest days a week and never put hard days back to back. Take an easy day when your legs feel heavy. Sleep is part of recovery, so keep regular bed and waking times through the plan.
- **Know where the impact is.** The pounding parts are the running and the burpee broad jumps. The sled, rower, SkiErg and carries involve no jumping or landing. While you learn, step back out of the burpee instead of jumping, and do some runs on grass or a treadmill. If you have osteoporosis or a high risk of fractures, [the NHS says](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/bone-health/keep-your-bones-strong-over-65/) you may need to be careful with high-impact exercise. Ask your GP or doctor before you start.
- **Share the load.** In doubles you choose who does what at each station. Give the heavier work to whoever is stronger at it on the day, not to whoever is braver.
- **Do not rely on stretching.** Stretch afterwards if it feels good. A 2014 review of 25 injury-prevention trials found that strength and balance training cut sports injuries, but stretching made no difference.

## If it goes wrong

- **Something does not feel right.** Stop, and get it checked rather than training through it.
- **A sprain or strain.** For the first two or three days the NHS advises [PRICE](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sprains-and-strains/): protect it, rest it, put ice on it for up to 20 minutes every two to three hours, use a bandage for support and keep it raised. See a GP if it gets worse or does not improve. In many areas of England you can refer yourself to NHS physiotherapy (MSK) services. In the US, see your doctor or a physical therapist. Get urgent help (NHS 111, or an urgent care clinic in the US) if you cannot put weight on it or the swelling keeps growing. Go to A&E (the ER) if you heard a crack, the limb looks out of shape, or it is numb or cold.
- **Chest pain, sudden breathlessness or collapse.** Call 999 (911 in the US) for chest pain that is tight or squeezing or that spreads to the arms, neck or jaw, for severe breathlessness, or if someone collapses. Do not drive yourself to hospital.
- **Sore knees on lunges or wall balls.** Cut the depth and the number, and check your form with a coach. Pain that lasts or sharpens needs a GP or physiotherapist.
- **Your partner is faster or slower.** Run at the slower pace and give the stronger partner more of the heavy stations. That is the point of doubles.
- **You miss a week.** Pick up where you left off. Do not double up.
- **The sled will not move.** Lower your hips, lean in and take short steps. Practice it in every session for two weeks.

## Track it

Write down your 1 km times, the time for each station and how you split the work with your partner. Every other week, compare one station with the last time. Note how you felt the next morning: it tells you whether to add weight or hold.

## What next

If you are getting going, make your first race a doubles or a relay with friends, where the work is shared, rather than a solo race. If you are already fit, try the full race on your own, or keep your partnership and aim for a faster time next season. Marshall and Wood built theirs with coaches and a Saturday group. Look for the same around you.
