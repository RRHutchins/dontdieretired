## Why this works

Debra Kennard drew up business plans for a bookshop that never worked financially. At 57 she spotted a vacant storefront of about 300 square feet on her walk to work in University City, Missouri, and Gold Shoe Books opened by September, St. Louis Magazine reported. She runs it alone, stocks it for the customers on her doorstep, and asks them what they want to see.

Three things in her story are worth copying. She kept the plan ready, so she could move when the right place turned up. She kept the shop small, which keeps the costs small. And she listens to customers and changes the stock. The cheapest way to find out whether people will buy is to sell to them, before you sign anything.

## Before you start

- **Pick one idea** and describe it in a sentence.
- **Keep your day job, pension income or other work for now.** This is a test, not a leap.
- **Know the rules where you live.** A market stall or pop-up may need a trader's licence or a pitch fee. In the UK, check with your council and register with HMRC (the UK tax office) if you start trading; in the US, check your city or county for a business licence and your state for sales tax collection. A talk with an adviser helps: in the US, SCORE has volunteer business mentors, and in the UK your council can point you to local business support.
- **Set a budget you can afford to lose** for stock, a table and a card reader, and do not go over it.
- **Money and law vary by country and by place.** This plan is general information, not financial or legal advice.

## The plan

| Week | Do this | How often | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (new to this) | Visit three shops or stalls like the one you imagine; ask each owner what sells and what surprised them | Three visits | Write down every answer the same day |
| 1 (already trading) | Pull your last three months' sales; list your ten best and ten worst lines | Once | Your own figures, not your impressions |
| 2 (new to this) | Write the one-page plan: who comes in, what they buy, what it costs each month to open the door | Once, then revise | If the sums do not work, change the idea, not the sums |
| 2 (already trading) | Choose one new line or one new outlet to test (a market, a pop-up, an online shop) | Once | Only one change, so you can tell what worked |
| 3 to 4 (new to this) | Book a market stall, pop-up or shelf in someone else's shop for two dates; buy a small first stock | Twice | Small stock: you can always buy more |
| 3 to 4 (already trading) | Set up the test with a target: sales, margin and hours | Once | Decide now what result means "keep it" |
| 5 to 6 (new to this) | Trade on both dates; record every sale, how many people stopped, and what they asked for | Two trading days | A card asking "What would you like to see?" does the market research for you |
| 5 to 6 (already trading) | Run the test for four weekends or four weeks | Weekly | Keep the records the same way every time |
| 7 (both) | Add up the results: sales, costs, hours and the most-asked-for things | Once | Divide profit by hours to see what you earned per hour |
| 8 (new to this) | Decide: trade again, change the idea, or stop | Once | A clear "no" after eight weeks is a good result |
| 8 (already trading) | Keep, change or drop the new line, and act on the three most common customer requests | Once | Write down why, for next year |

## If it goes wrong

- **Nobody stops.** Change one thing at a time: the display, the price, the pitch or the day. Then try again.
- **People look but do not buy.** Ask them, kindly, what they were hoping to find. Their answers are your next stock order.
- **The numbers never add up.** That is what happened to Kennard's plans for years. Keep the plan, keep it current, and watch for a cheaper space or a different model, such as a stall, a shared shop or a trailer.

## Track it

Keep one notebook or spreadsheet: date, place, hours, sales, costs, and the three things people asked for most. In week 8 you will have the evidence to decide.

## What next

If the test worked, trade regularly for three months before you look at a lease, and keep a sign up asking customers what they would like to see.
