## Why this works

Prehabilitation, getting stronger before an operation, is now recommended by many surgical teams for hip and knee replacements. The muscles around a joint do the walking and stair-climbing afterwards, and the stronger they are on the day, the sooner those come back. Trials of pre-surgery exercise have reported shorter hospital stays and quicker return to independent walking.

You do not need an operation booked for this to be worth doing. Leg and hip strength keeps you off the floor and out of the chair. Older adults who take up resistance training gain measurable strength within six to eight weeks, and beginners improve fastest.

The plan is narrow on purpose: three movements, twice a week, one small increase each week. A written log turns "I think I'm stronger" into "4 kg more than week one", and the number keeps people turning up.

## Before you start

- Check with your GP first. If surgery is coming, ask the consultant or physiotherapist: "What exercise would make this go better?" Take this plan along.
- Kit: a sturdy chair, a rucksack you can add tins to, a resistance band, and later a kettlebell or dumbbells (£20–£40).
- Cost: one session with a physiotherapist or trainer who works with over-60s, £40–£70, is the best money in this plan. NHS physiotherapy may be available if surgery is booked. Council gyms run over-60s inductions, often free.
- Where: search "strength and balance class" plus your town; [Age UK](https://www.ageuk.org.uk) and [Later Life Training](https://www.laterlifetraining.co.uk) list instructors. (In the US, SilverSneakers classes or a physical therapist.)

## The plan

The three movements: **sit-to-stand** (squat), **hip hinge** (the deadlift pattern, bag first, kettlebell later), and **row** (band or dumbbell).

| Week | Do this | How often | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sit-to-stand, hands on knees, 2 sets of 5. Hinge with an empty bag, 2 × 8. Band row, 2 × 8. | 2 sessions, 15 minutes | Never done any of this? Higher chair, 3 reps not 5. That counts. |
| 2 | Same, add 1 rep to each set. Book the physio or trainer for week 3. | 2 sessions | Write down every set. |
| 3 | Have all three movements checked by the professional. | 1 session plus 1 at home | A straight back is the whole hinge. |
| 4 | 3 sets of each. Two tins in the bag. Hands off knees if you can. | 2 sessions, 20 minutes | Muscle ache next day is normal. Joint pain is not; back off and ask. |
| 5 | Swap the bag for a 6–8 kg kettlebell. Row with a heavier band or 3–5 kg dumbbell. | 2 sessions | At a gym: the same with a trap bar, with an instructor. |
| 6 | Add a rep or 1–2 kg to each movement. Sit-to-stand holding a weight at your chest. | 2 sessions | One increase per movement per week, no more. |
| 7 | Same again. Try a lower chair or a slower descent (3 seconds down). | 2 sessions | Slow lowering is where much of the strength is built. |
| 8 | Re-test the Track it table. Compare with week 1. | 2 sessions | Then keep going. |

## If it goes wrong

- **Knees hurt on the sit-to-stand.** Higher seat, shorter range, and ask the physio. Pain that builds is a signal to stop.
- **Lower back aches after the hinge.** A rounded back or too much weight. Back to the empty bag; film yourself from the side.
- **You keep missing the second session.** Same two diary slots every week, paired with something you already do.
- **Surgery arrives before week 8.** Good; you are stronger than you were. Stop when the surgeon says, restart with their physio, then come back to week 1.
