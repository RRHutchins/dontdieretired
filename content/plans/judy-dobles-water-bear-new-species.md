## Why this works

Judy Dobles did not set out to discover a species. She came to tardigrades through other volunteer work, monarch monitoring, invasive species tracking and iNaturalist, then trained in them and sampled more than 143 sites at Cumming Nature Center over two years. The new species, *Mesocrista ojagwaji*, came out of that steady, recorded looking.

This plan copies the method, not the outcome. A new species cannot be promised. What you can count on is the skill of preparing a sample, the pleasure of watching an animal most people never see, and a record of what lives where you live.

## Before you start

- **A microscope.** A stereo (dissecting) microscope is the right tool for spotting water bears; the Quekett Microscopical Club says these magnify from about 6x to 60x. The Minnesota Department of Natural Resources' guide starts at 15x and moves to 30x. If you do not own one, ask a school, college, nature center or microscopy club whether you can borrow or use one.
- **Distilled water** (not tap water), a few shallow dishes such as Petri dishes, a dropper or pipette, and brown paper lunch bags for samples.
- **Somewhere to sample.** Your own garden is easiest. Anywhere else, take only a small piece and ask permission, and never collect on nature reserves without it.
- **A notebook or spreadsheet** with columns for date, place, what the sample was (moss, lichen, which surface), hours soaked, and what you saw.
- **Hygiene.** Wash your hands after handling samples and do not eat or drink while you work, as the Minnesota guide advises.

## The plan

| Week | Do this | How often | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (new to this) | Collect one palm-sized piece of moss, bag and label it. Soak it in about 20 mL of distilled water for four to eight hours, then scan the dish at 15x for movement and zoom to 30x. | 2 sessions | Look for eight stubby legs with claws and a slow, crawling walk. Worm-like nematodes and tube-shaped rotifers are common too; note them. |
| 1 (already doing it) | Choose ten sites within walking distance: different mosses, lichens, logs, walls and roofs. Collect and label the first five. | 2 sessions | Record the surface, how wet it was and whether it was in sun or shade. |
| 2 (new to this) | Try three different mosses (a wall, a log, a tree trunk). Soak each for a full eight hours this time and compare. | 3 sessions | Dried tardigrades can take four to eight hours to revive, so patience pays. |
| 2 (already doing it) | Process the first five samples and collect the other five. Photograph through the eyepiece with a phone if you can. | 3 sessions | Keep each dish labeled; mix-ups ruin a survey. |
| 3 (new to this) | Learn to tell water bears from the other animals in the dish. Read the Quekett Microscopical Club's beginners' pages and the Minnesota guide again. | 2 sessions | Count how many tardigrades each sample holds, even if the number is zero. |
| 3 (already doing it) | Process the last five samples. Make a simple table of which sites had tardigrades and how many. | 3 sessions | Look for patterns: wet against dry, sun against shade. |
| 4 (both) | Share it. Show a friend, grandchild or club a living water bear, then put the animals back in their dish, and either dispose of the used moss or tuck it into a corner of the garden, as the Minnesota guide suggests. Decide on next month's goal. | Once, plus one session | A microscopy club, natural history society or nature center is the place to take your notes. |

## If it goes wrong

- **Nothing moves.** Soak longer, up to eight hours, and try another sample from a different surface. Some samples hold none at all.
- **You cannot tell what you are seeing.** Count the legs. Eight legs with claws is a tardigrade; anything worm-like or tube-like is something else, and worth noting anyway.
- **The image is blurred or dark.** Use less water in the dish, adjust the light from below and above, and focus slowly from low power.
- **You have no microscope.** Ask before you buy. Clubs such as the Quekett let members use club-owned microscopes.
- **It feels like homework.** Drop to one sample a week. This should be the most absorbing hour of the week, not a chore.

## Track it

Each week, write down samples taken, hours soaked, tardigrades seen and anything you could not identify. By the end of week 4 you should be able to say: "I found water bears in ___ of my ___ samples, and next month I will ___."

## What next

- **Learn to name them.** The Eagle Hill Institute in Maine ran an online Introduction to Tardigrades on Zoom in early 2026, covering collecting and identification to genus; check its seminars page for the next one.
- **Find company.** In the UK, the Quekett Microscopical Club meets roughly monthly at the Natural History Museum in London, with many meetings on Zoom. In the US, ask your nearest nature center or natural history museum about volunteering and citizen science.
- **Keep going.** Judy Dobles's survey ran for two years. Return to your best sites each season and see what changes.
