## Why this works

The evidence on late-starting founders is better than the folklore. A 2020 study by researchers at MIT, Northwestern and the US Census Bureau found the average age of founders of the fastest-growing new firms was 45, and that founders in their fifties did better than those in their thirties. Experience, contacts and a realistic view of risk count, and by 50 you have all three.

Successful late starters start small and alongside. A side venture funded by time rather than savings can fail cheaply. So: no registration, premises or lease until strangers have paid you ten times.

The other principle is strangers. Friends buy anything once. The question is whether a stranger pays and comes back; twelve weeks answers it.

## Before you start

- No GP needed, but talk to an accountant before registering anything. Nothing here is financial advice.
- Kit: a notebook, a way to take payment (card reader £20–£40, or bank transfer) and whatever your thing needs in its smallest form.
- Cost: aim to spend under £200 in twelve weeks. A market pitch is £15–£40 a day; a craft fair table £10–£30.
- Where: your council's markets team for pitch prices; [Flowers from the Farm](https://www.flowersfromthefarm.co.uk) if it is flowers; the free [Business Support Helpline](https://www.gov.uk/business-support-helpline); [Enterprise Nation](https://www.enterprisenation.com) for over-50 founders. (In the US, SCORE mentoring, staffed largely by retired business people, and SBA Small Business Development Centers.)
- If you are employed, check your contract for anything about outside work.

## The plan

| Week | Do this | How often | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Write the ten-year sentence: "If I had ten working years left I would spend them on ___." Then the smallest paid version: one bunch, one hour, one lesson. | One sitting | Never sold anything? Good. The smallest version should feel embarrassingly small. |
| 2 | Talk to five people who buy things like this (not friends). Ask what they pay and what annoys them. | 5 conversations | Not selling yet. Listening. |
| 3 | Make the first ten units. Price by looking at what three others charge. | Evenings and one weekend day | Do not undercut. Cheap signals unsure. |
| 4 | Sell the first three: a market stall, a noticeboard, a local Facebook group. | One selling day | Note who bought and why. |
| 5–6 | Sell seven more. Ask every buyer: "Would you buy this again, and what would make it better?" | One selling day a week | Ten strangers paid is the test passed. |
| 7–8 | Fix the two most common complaints. Ask the first ten buyers if they want more. | One selling day a week | Repeat buyers are the whole business. |
| 9–10 | Work out the numbers: cost and hours per unit, price, what a year of Saturdays would earn. | One evening, then a call to an accountant | A disappointing number is information, not a verdict. |
| 11 | Decide: keep as a sideline, grow it, or shelve it and test the next idea. Write the reason down. | One sitting | All three are wins. |
| 12 | If keeping: register with HMRC as a sole trader (free), open a separate account, book four selling dates. If shelving: week 1, idea two. | One afternoon | Give the seconds to a hospice or food bank. |

## If it goes wrong

- **Nobody buys in week 4.** Usually the place, not the product. Try a different market; ask the week 2 people where they shop.
- **Too busy for the selling day.** Go fortnightly rather than dropping it.
- **Enjoying it but losing money.** Raise the price 20 per cent and see who leaves. Often nobody.
- **You have talked yourself out of it.** Do week 1 anyway. The sentence takes ten minutes and commits you to nothing.
