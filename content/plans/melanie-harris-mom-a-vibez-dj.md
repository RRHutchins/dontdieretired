## Why this works

Melanie Harris had been on the dancefloor since the late 1980s before she moved behind the decks at 55. Within a year, the Express & Star reported, she had two house mixes played on UK radio, had played live sets around the West Midlands and had a paid festival booking in Birmingham through Selextorhood, a DJ community.

Three things helped her, and this plan copies them. She started with music she knew. She practised. And she found people already doing it, who gave her somewhere to play. DJing is a listening skill: you improve by doing short sessions often, recording yourself and listening back, not by buying more equipment.

## Before you start

- **You do not need your own decks yet.** Most beginners' courses supply the decks and mixer. Our guide to [learning to DJ](/list/learn-to-dj/) lists weekend and weekly courses in the UK and US; check current prices and dates with the course.
- **Headphones** that close around your ears let you hear the next track over the speakers. A USB stick holds your music for class.
- **If you want to practise at home**, a beginner's DJ controller and free or bundled software on a laptop is the cheapest way in. Try a course first so you know what you need.
- **Use music you own.** Check that the tracks you want to play will load into the software or onto the stick your course uses.
- **Look after your hearing.** Keep headphone and speaker volume moderate, and wear earplugs in clubs.

## The plan

| Week | Do this | How often | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (new to this) | Choose 20 tracks you love in one style; book a taster or course | Once | Write down the tempo (BPM) of each track |
| 1 (already mixing) | Record a 30-minute mix as you play now | Once | Listen next day and note your three worst transitions |
| 2 to 3 (new to this) | Course sessions or home practice: beatmatching by ear | 30 minutes, four days a week | One skill at a time. Use two tracks of a similar tempo |
| 2 to 3 (already mixing) | Work on the three weak transitions; learn phrasing (mixing at the start of a 32-beat phrase) | 30 to 45 minutes, four days a week | Count the phrases out loud at first |
| 4 to 5 (new to this) | Add EQ: swap the bass from one track to the other | 30 minutes, four days a week | Never have two basslines running at full at the same time |
| 4 to 5 (already mixing) | Build a 45-minute set with a clear shape: warm-up, peak, wind-down | Three or four sessions | Practise it start to finish twice |
| 6 (new to this) | Record your first 15-minute mix | Once, plus practice | Listen back the next day; it will sound better than you expect |
| 6 (already mixing) | Find a collective, community radio station or bar with new-DJ or open-deck nights | Once | Send a short mix and ask for a slot |
| 7 to 8 (new to this) | Record a 30-minute mix and share it with a friend | Practice four days a week | Ask them which bit they liked best |
| 7 to 8 (already mixing) | Play your 45-minute set to people | Once | Watch the floor, not the screen |

## If it goes wrong

- **The beats drift apart.** Normal for weeks. Use tracks with steady drums, keep practising by ear, and check the tempo display only to confirm.
- **It all sounds muddy.** Too much bass from both tracks at once. Cut the bass on the incoming track until the switch.
- **You freeze when people are watching.** Play to one friend, then a small group. Have your first three tracks planned.
- **No courses near you.** Online lessons and a home controller work; join an online group so someone hears your mixes.

## Track it

Keep a list of every mix you record with the date, length and one thing that improved. Save the recordings: in week 8, listen to your first one again.

## What next

Keep recording a mix every fortnight. Look for the community around the music you love, as Harris did with Selextorhood, and play wherever there is a chance: a friend's party, a community event, a radio slot. And keep going out to hear other DJs; that is where the next idea comes from.
