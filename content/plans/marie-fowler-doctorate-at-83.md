## Why this works

The fear of "not retaining information" is rarely borne out when tested. Processing speed slows with age, but vocabulary, reasoning and the ability to connect new knowledge to old hold up well into the eighties. Older learners often do better on depth because they have more to connect things to.

What matters more is structure. Memory at any age is built by spaced, repeated retrieval: a short daily session beats a long weekly one, and testing yourself beats re-reading. Hence the 20-minute daily slot and the weekly "teach someone" step.

The other reason is the size of the promise. One term, not a qualification. A ten-week experiment is too small for doubt to argue with, and by week three the retention question has answered itself.

## Before you start

- No GP needed. If you have hearing or sight difficulties, tell the provider; adjustments are standard.
- Kit: notebook, pen, a quiet chair, and the phone in another room. A laptop or tablet if online; libraries lend them.
- Cost: [FutureLearn](https://www.futurelearn.com) and [OpenLearn](https://www.open.edu/openlearn) are free; U3A membership £10–£40 a year; local adult education £50–£200 a term with over-60s concessions; the [Open University](https://www.open.ac.uk) charges per module. (In the US, community colleges often waive tuition for over-60s; OLLI programmes are the U3A equivalent; Coursera and edX online.)
- Where: [u3a.org.uk](https://www.u3a.org.uk), your council's adult learning service, the library noticeboard, and the [WEA](https://www.wea.org.uk).
- Choose a subject you are curious about, not one you should do. Curiosity is the fuel for week six.

## The plan

| Week | Do this | How often | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Shortlist three courses starting within a month. Enrol on one. Fix your 20-minute slot (same time, same chair). | One afternoon, then daily | Not been in a classroom for decades? Choose a course with a live tutor and a small group; the human contact is what carries you. |
| 2 | First class or first online unit. In your slot: read or watch, then write three sentences on what you learned. | Daily 20 min, plus the class | Three sentences is the whole task. Not notes: sentences. |
| 3 | Teach one thing from the course to someone, out loud, this week. | Daily slot, plus one conversation | The retention question gets answered here. |
| 4–5 | Add self-testing: at the start of each slot, write what you remember from yesterday before opening anything. | Daily slot | Two minutes of recall, eighteen of new material. |
| 6 | Mid-term slump week. Do the minimum: the slot, the class, nothing more. Book a coffee with a classmate. | Daily slot | Everybody dips here. Turning up is the whole job this week. |
| 7–8 | Start the piece of work (essay, presentation, project, or the course's final quiz). Break it into 20-minute chunks. | Daily slot | Ask the tutor for feedback on a rough draft; that is what they are for. |
| 9 | Finish and submit or complete it. | Daily slot | Done beats perfect. |
| 10 | Review: what did you enjoy, what would you do next? Look at the next term's list. | One sitting | Decide term by term. That is the method. |

## If it goes wrong

- **You forget most of it between sessions.** Normal. Move the recall exercise to the start of every slot and shorten what you read. Retention is a skill that returns with practice.
- **The slot keeps getting eaten.** Move it to first thing in the morning, before the day makes claims on you.
- **The course is the wrong level.** Tell the tutor by week 3. Moving down is a good decision, not a failure; moving up is often possible.
- **Technology is the barrier.** Libraries and Age UK run free digital skills sessions. Or pick a course in a room, with paper.
