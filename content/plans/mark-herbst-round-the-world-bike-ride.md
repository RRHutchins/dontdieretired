## Why this works

Mark Herbst had raced for years before he rode around the world, and he said racing had cost him something: he had ridden past the entrance to the Grand Canyon four times without seeing it. His round-the-world ride, 30,000 km (18,641 miles) through 25 countries in 324 days, was built on a different idea: long, steady days, carrying everything, with time to look around. Global News reported he rode between eight and 12 hours a day.

Touring rewards the same thing at any scale. It is not about speed. It is about riding at a pace you can hold all day, then doing it again tomorrow. This plan builds exactly that: time in the saddle, back-to-back days and the skill of travelling light, in small steps.

## Before you start

- **Check with a GP (family doctor) first** if you have a heart, joint or back problem, or have not exercised for some time.
- **Get the bike checked.** A bike shop service before you start, with attention to brakes, tyres and gears, saves trouble on the road. Learn to fix a puncture at home before you need to do it at the roadside.
- **Fit matters more than kit.** If your hands, neck or knees ache after an hour, ask a shop to check saddle height and position before you add distance.
- **Carry less than you think.** A rack and two panniers, or a frame and saddle bag, are steadier than a heavy rucksack once you ride more than a few hours.
- **Plan on signed routes.** Our guide to [riding a long-distance cycle route](/list/long-distance-cycle-route/) links to the National Cycle Network route finder in the UK and the Adventure Cycling Association's maps in the US.

## The plan

| Week | Do this | How often | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (getting going) | Ride 15 to 25 km (10 to 15 miles) on quiet roads or paths | Twice | Conversation pace. Stop for a drink halfway |
| 1 (already fit) | Two rides of 50 to 60 km and one of 80 km (50 miles) | Three times | Ride the longest one with your bags packed as for a tour |
| 2 to 3 (getting going) | Build one ride to 35 km (about 22 miles); keep a shorter mid-week ride | Twice weekly | Practise eating and drinking on the bike before you feel you need to |
| 2 to 3 (already fit) | Back-to-back days: 80 km then 60 km, loaded | One weekend each week, plus one shorter ride | Note how your legs, hands and back feel on day two |
| 4 (getting going) | Your first night away: ride 25 to 30 km to a B&B, campsite or friend's, ride home next day | Once, plus one short ride | Carry only what you need for one night |
| 4 (already fit) | Two days of 100 km (60 miles), loaded | Once | Eat every hour. Finish each day with energy to spare |
| 5 to 6 (getting going) | Build your longest ride to 45 to 50 km (about 30 miles); add a hilly route | Twice weekly | Use your lowest gears early on climbs |
| 5 to 6 (already fit) | A week of four riding days totalling 300 km (about 190 miles) | Once in the fortnight | One day of at least 120 km (75 miles). Take the next day off |
| 7 to 8 (getting going) | A two-night trip of 30 to 40 km a day on a signed route | Once | Book your stops in advance. Leave time to look round |
| 7 to 8 (already fit) | A three- to four-day tour of 70 to 100 km a day | Once | This is the dress rehearsal for a 10- to 14-day tour |

## If it goes wrong

- **Sore knees.** Usually saddle height or pushing too high a gear. Lower the gear, spin faster and get your fit checked. If pain persists, stop and see your GP.
- **Numb hands or a sore neck.** Change hand position often; ask a shop to look at handlebar height and reach.
- **Saddle soreness.** Padded shorts, no underwear beneath them, and a day off the bike. Build distance more slowly.
- **Bad weather on a planned day.** Swap days or shorten the ride. Know where the trains or buses run on your route.
- **Day two feels terrible.** That is what back-to-back training is for. Ride it slower; it gets easier with each repeat.

## Track it

Keep a note of the date, distance, time on the bike, the weight you carried and one thing you saw. On back-to-back weekends, rate how you felt on day two from one to five. By week 8 that number should be climbing.

## What next

Choose a tour of a week or two, somewhere you have always meant to go: a signed national route, a coast or a crossing between two cities. Plan days no longer than your longest training day, with a rest day every four or five. And do as Herbst did: leave time to stop and see the place you rode all that way to reach.
