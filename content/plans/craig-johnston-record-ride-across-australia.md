## Why this works

Craig Johnston crossed Australia at about 140 km (88 miles) a day for 44 days. Nothing about that is a sprint. Long-distance riding is built from three plain things: time in the saddle, days ridden back to back, and being able to look after yourself and the bike.

This plan trains those three in that order. Each week adds a little time, then a second day, then luggage. The overnight ride at the end is short enough to be fun and long enough to teach you what a tour feels like.

The health case needs far less than Johnston's mileage. In a University of Glasgow study of 264,337 people, published in the BMJ in 2017, those who cycled to work, averaging about 30 miles (48 km) a week, had a 41 per cent lower risk of dying early over five years than commuters who did not walk or cycle. It was an observational study, so it shows a link and not a cause. By week four of either track you will be riding more than that.

## Before you start

- **Check with your GP (family doctor)** if you have a heart condition, chest pain, dizziness, or have not exercised for some time.
- **The bike:** any bike that fits you and has working brakes and gears. Have a shop check it if it has been in the shed for years. An e-bike is a perfectly good way to do the getting-going track.
- **Kit:** helmet, lights, a pump, two spare inner tubes, tyre levers, a multi-tool, a water bottle. Padded shorts earn their keep after an hour.
- **Luggage (from week 5):** a rack and panniers, or strap-on bikepacking bags. Borrow before you buy.
- **Route:** traffic-free paths and quiet lanes. In the UK look at the National Cycle Network; in the US, rail trails.
- **One skill:** fix a puncture at home before week 2. Johnston had mechanical trouble in the desert with nobody to ring.

## The plan

| Week | Do this | How often | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (getting going) | Ride 30–45 minutes on a flat, traffic-free route at talking pace. | 2 rides | Note the distance. That is your starting number. |
| 1 (already fit) | Ride 60 km (37 miles) steady, plus one 90-minute ride. | 2–3 rides | Eat something every hour from the start. |
| 2 (getting going) | One ride of 45 minutes, one of an hour with a café stop. | 2 rides | A destination makes distance easier. |
| 2 (already fit) | 80 km (50 miles), plus two 90-minute rides. | 3 rides | Practise the puncture repair by the roadside, timed. |
| 3 (getting going) | 10 miles (16 km) and 12 miles (19 km). | 2–3 rides | Sore sit bones settle by the third week. Sharp knee pain means the saddle height needs checking. |
| 3 (already fit) | 100 km (62 miles) in one ride. | 3 rides | Hold a pace you could keep for another two hours. |
| 4 (getting going) | 15 miles (24 km) on Saturday, 8 miles (13 km) on Sunday. | 2–3 rides | Your first back-to-back days. |
| 4 (already fit) | 100 km Saturday, 80 km (50 miles) Sunday. | 3 rides | The second morning is the lesson. Note what hurts. |
| 5 (getting going) | Fit the luggage. Ride 12 miles (19 km) with it loaded. | 2 rides | A loaded bike is slower to turn and to stop. |
| 5 (already fit) | 110 km (68 miles) fully loaded, including the tent if you will camp. | 3 rides | Weigh the kit. Leave a third of it at home. |
| 6 (getting going) | 20 miles (32 km) loaded. Book the overnight stop. | 2–3 rides | A pub, a hostel or a campsite about 20 miles from home. |
| 6 (already fit) | 140 km (88 miles) in a day: Johnston's daily average. | 2–3 rides | Start early. Plan around shops that exist. |
| 7 | Easier week for both tracks: two short rides and a full check of the bike. | 2 rides | Tyres, brake pads, chain, lights. |
| 8 | The overnight. Getting going: 20 miles out, sleep, 20 miles home. Already fit: 120 km (75 miles) out, sleep, 120 km home. | 1 weekend | No lifts and no van. Carry it all, as Johnston did. |

## If it goes wrong

- **You miss a week.** Repeat the last week you finished. Fitness on a bike comes back quickly.
- **Headwind or rain on the long day.** Shorten the loop and keep the time. Johnston had torrential rain and headwinds and kept moving.
- **Numb hands or a sore neck.** Raise the handlebars or have a shop look at your position. Small changes make a large difference.
- **Knee pain.** A saddle that is too low is the usual cause. If it persists, rest and see a physiotherapist or your GP.
- **The overnight falls through.** Do the two days from home and sleep in your own bed. The riding is what counts.
- **Traffic frightens you.** Stay on traffic-free routes. Many councils and clubs run free adult cycle-confidence sessions.

## What next

- Getting going? Repeat week 8 with a second night, or join a club's social ride. Already fit? Enter a 200 km (124-mile) audax or brevet, or plan a week-long unsupported route.
- Johnston's next target is the Tour Divide, 2,700 miles (4,300 km) from Canada to the Mexican border. Yours might be one long-distance cycle route ridden end to end.
