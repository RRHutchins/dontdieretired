In December 1843 Henry Cole had a pile of letters he had not answered. Cole was a British civil servant (a government official) who had helped bring in the Penny Post, the cheap postage that made sending letters affordable.

He asked his friend, the artist John Callcott Horsley, to design a printed card instead. It showed three generations of a family raising a glass, with scenes of charity on either side. There was space to write "To" and "From".

A thousand were printed. Cole sold the ones he did not need for a shilling each (an old British coin). The picture of children drinking wine caused a stir with the temperance movement, which campaigned against alcohol.

Museums disagree on whether it sold well. The V&A in London calls it a commercial flop because it was expensive, while The Postal Museum says the row did not stop people buying. What came next is clearer: the V&A says about a billion Christmas cards are now bought in the UK each year.
