In February 1930 Clyde Tombaugh was 24 and had never been to college. He had grown up on a farm in Kansas and built his own telescopes.

At Lowell Observatory, a center for astronomy in Flagstaff, Arizona, his job was to photograph the same patch of sky a few nights apart. Then he flipped between the two pictures in a machine called a blink comparator. Distant stars stay put. Anything that shifts is much closer.

On February 18 one faint dot shifted a little. It was Pluto, a world beyond Neptune.

The day after the discovery was announced, an 11-year-old girl in Oxford, England, suggested its name over breakfast.

Would you have had the patience for that search?
